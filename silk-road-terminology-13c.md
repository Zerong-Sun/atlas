# 13th-Century Silk Road Administrative & Cultural Terminology

> A historically verified reference table mapping 10 administrative/cultural concepts across 5 regions along the Silk Road during the 13th century. Terminology sourced from academic sources (Encyclopaedia Iranica, Cambridge History of the Mongol Empire, specialist monographs, and primary source references), cross-checked via web search.

---

## 1. Customs House / Tax Office (替代"衙门")

| Region | Term (Transliteration) | Original Script | Language | Historical Note |
|--------|----------------------|-----------------|----------|-----------------|
| **West Asia** (Ilkhanate) | *tamghā-khāna* / *dīwān-i tamghā* | تمغاخانه / ديوان تمغا | Persian/Arabic | The *tamghā-khāna* was the office where the Mongol-introduced *tamghā* commercial tax was collected. By the 14th century, Tabriz had two tamghā offices: one for silk/satin/spices from China, another for European and Egyptian goods. The *dīwān-i istīfā* was the overarching ministry of finance. |
| **Central Asia** (Chagatai) | *tamghā-khāna* / *bāj-gāh* | تمغاخانه / باجگاه | Persian/Turkic | Same Mongol-derived institution. In Samarkand and Bukhara, the tamghā was levied at gates and caravanserais on transiting Silk Road goods. Kebek Khan's reforms (early 14th c.) standardized the tax collection system. |
| **Steppe** (Mongol) | *al-tamghā* office / *tamghā-khāna* | آل تمغا | Mongolian/Persian | The *al-tamghā* ("red seal") was both the supreme seal and the tax authority. The *tamghā* tax originated here and was then exported empire-wide. The red seal office validated commercial and administrative documents. |
| **India** (Delhi Sultanate) | *dīwān-i wizārat* / *shahna-i mandī* | ديوان وزارت / شحنه مندى | Persian | The *dīwān-i wizārat* was the central finance ministry headed by the *wazīr*. The *shahna-i mandī* (market supervisor) oversaw market taxation. Customs at ports fell under the *shāh-bandar* (harbor master). |
| **Maritime Asia** (Sumatra/Java) | *syahbandar* (later) / port *pājeg* | شاه بندر | Malay/Javanese via Persian | In Majapahit Java (1293+), the *rakryān kanuruhan* (high chancellor) handled foreign merchants and collected trade duties — the functional predecessor to the *syahbandar* (harbor master). In Samudera Pasai (Sumatra, c. 1267+), the *syahbandar* was already in use. |

## 2. Official / Clerk / Administrator (替代"官"/"吏")

| Region | Term (Transliteration) | Original Script | Language | Historical Note |
|--------|----------------------|-----------------|----------|-----------------|
| **West Asia** (Ilkhanate) | *mustawfī* / *bitikči* / *'āmil* / *kātib* / *nā'ib* | مستوفی / بیتیكچی / عامل / كاتب / نائب | Persian, Mongol, Arabic | *Mustawfī* = accountant in the *dīwān-i istīfā*. *Bitikči* (Mongol/Turkic) = scribe, a key chancery official. *'Āmil* = tax collector. *Kātib* = clerk. *Nā'ib* = deputy. The Ilkhanid bureaucracy combined Mongol overseers with Persian administrative professionals. |
| **Central Asia** (Chagatai) | *bitikči* / *'āmil* / *mutasarrif* | بیتیكچی / عامل / متصرف | Turkic/Persian/Arabic | *Bitikči*, the Mongol scribe title, was standard here. *'Āmil* and *mutasarrif* were Persianate revenue officials. Local Uyghur scribes dominated the chancery, writing in Uyghur script even for Persian documents. |
| **Steppe** (Mongol) | *darughachi* / *bitikči* / *yarghuchi* | ᠳᠠᠷᠤᠭᠠᠴᠢ / ᠪᠢᠴᠢᠬᠡᠴᠢ (Mongol script) | Mongolian | *Darughachi* (pl. *darugha*) was the chief local administrator — the khan's direct representative, supervising tax collection and enforcing decrees. *Bitikči* = scribe/secretary. *Yarghuchi* = judge. *Noyan* = commander/lord. |
| **India** (Delhi Sultanate) | *mustawfī-i mamālik* / *mushrif-i mamālik* / *'āmil* / *kārkun* / *wazīr* | مستوفی ممالک / مشرف ممالک / عامل / کارکن / وزیر | Persian/Arabic | *Mustawfī-i mamālik* = Auditor General (expenditure oversight). *Mushrif-i mamālik* = Accountant General (income records). *Wazīr* = chief minister heading the *dīwān-i wizārat*. *'Āmil* = revenue collector. *Kārkun* = clerk. *Shiqdār* = officer over a *shiq* (sub-provincial district). *Kōtwāl* = city police chief. |
| **Maritime Asia** (Sumatra/Java) | *rakryān* / *dātu* / *patih* / *tumenggung* | (Old Javanese) / (Malay) | Old Javanese, Malay | *Rakryān* = provincial governor in Majapahit. *Dātu* = chief/ruler in Srivijaya and Malay polities. *Patih* = prime minister. *Tumenggung* = military commander. *Demung* and *rangga* = titles for ranking officials. In Pasai, Persianate titles entered via Islam. |

## 3. Tax / Tariff on Goods (替代"关税")

| Region | Term (Transliteration) | Original Script | Language | Historical Note |
|--------|----------------------|-----------------|----------|-----------------|
| **West Asia** (Ilkhanate) | *tamghā* / *bāj* / *kharāj* | تمغا / باج / خراج | Mongol/Persian/Arabic | *Tamghā* = the Mongol commercial tax, typically 5-10% on goods, replacing the Islamic *zakāt* (2.5%). Levied at roads and ports. *Bāj* = transit toll/customs duty (Persian), averaging 2-3%. *Kharāj* = Islamic land tax. Under Ghazan Khan's reforms (1295-1304), tamghā was halved in some towns. Foreign merchants in Tabriz paid only 3.5%. |
| **Central Asia** (Chagatai) | *tamghā* / *bāj* / *zakāt* | تمغا / باج / زكات | Mongolic/Persian/Arabic | Same tamghā/bāj system. Chagatai khans' decrees mention *tamghā* levies. The tax was paid at city gates and along caravan routes. *Zakāt* (2.5%) was used for Muslim traders, while non-Muslims paid *tamghā*. |
| **Steppe** (Mongol) | *tamghā* / *qūbchūr* / *qalān* | тамга / ᠬᠤᠪᠴᠢᠷ | Mongolian | *Tamghā* = commercial tax, the origin of the system. *Qūbchūr* (qubchir) = poll tax on nomadic herdsmen (1 animal per 100). *Qalān* = tribute from conquered peoples. The *tamghā* tax was one of Mongol Empire's most distinctive fiscal innovations — exported to all successor khanates. In the Yuan period, paper money (*zhōngtǒng chāo* 中統鈔) replaced much cash taxation. |
| **India** (Delhi Sultanate) | *kharāj* / *jizya* / *'ushr* / *zakāt* | خراج / جزية / عشر / زكات | Arabic/Persian | *Kharāj* = land tax (the largest state revenue). *Jizya* = poll tax on non-Muslims (dhimmis). *'Ushr* = tithe on Muslim-owned land (10%). *Zakāt* = alms tax on Muslims (2.5% on goods). *Bāj* = customs. The fiscal terminology was Islamic, not Mongol — the Delhi Sultanate was outside the Chinggisid tax tradition. |
| **Maritime Asia** (Sumatra/Java) | *pājeg* / *drewya haji* | (Old Javanese) | Old Javanese | *Pājeg* = tax/tribute in Old Javanese. *Drewya haji* = "royal property/revenue" — state income from ports, markets, and vassals. Port duties were the lifeline of Srivijaya and later Majapahit. In Majapahit, the *rakryān kanuruhan* collected trade duties from foreign merchants. Chinese sources report port officials levied fees on incoming ships. |

## 4. Religious Buildings

| Region | Term (Transliteration) | Original Script | Religion | Historical Note |
|--------|----------------------|-----------------|----------|-----------------|
| **West Asia** | *masjid-i jāmi'* | مسجد جامع | Islam (Sunni/Shi'i) | The *masjid-i jāmi'* (congregational/Friday mosque) was the central religious and social focal point of every major city, often paired with the bāzār and madrasa. Under the Ilkhans, especially after Ghazan's conversion (1295), massive waqf-funded mosque-madrasa complexes were built. |
| | *kanīsa* / *bī'a* | كنيسة / بيعة | Nestorian Christianity | *Kanīsa* (Arabic) or *bī'a* (Syriac/Arabic) = church. The Ilkhanate had significant Nestorian communities, especially under Hülegü (whose wife Doquz Khatun was Nestorian). The Nestorian catholicos maintained a seat at Maragha. |
| | *khanqāh* / *zāwiya* | خانقاه / زاویه | Sufism | *Khānaqāh* = Sufi lodge, a key institution in Ilkhanid religious life, often part of waqf complexes alongside mosques and madrasas. |
| **Central Asia** | *masjid* / *masjid-i jāmi'* | مسجد / مسجد جامع | Islam | Samarkand and Bukhara had major congregational mosques, funded through waqf endowments. Under later Chagatai khans who converted to Islam (Tarmashirin, 1326-1334), mosque construction accelerated. |
| | *madrasa* | مدرسه | Islamic education | Madrasas were increasingly built, often attached to mosques. The waqf system sustained them. |
| | *but-khāna* / *bahār* / *farxār* | بتخانه / بهار / فرخار | Buddhism (remnant) | *But-khāna* (Persian "idol-house") = Buddhist monastery. *Bahār* (from Sanskrit *vihāra*) and *farxār* (Sogdian adaptation) were terms for Buddhist monasteries still remembered in the 13th c., particularly the famous *Nawbahār* ("New Monastery") at Balkh. |
| **Steppe** (Mongol) | *süme* / *keyid* | ᠰᠦᠮᠡ (Mongol script) | Buddhism | *Süme* = temple, *keyid* = monastery. Tibetan Buddhism (Lamaism) gained favor at the Yuan court under Kublai Khan. The Phags-pa Lama was appointed imperial preceptor. |
| | *Yělikěwēn jiào* / *Shízì Sì* | 也里可溫教 / 十字寺 | Nestorian Christianity | *Yělikěwēn* (derived from Mongol/Turkic *ärkä'ün*) = the official Yuan term for Christians. The *Chóngfú Sì* (崇福司, "Administration of Honored Blessing") was established in 1289 to oversee Nestorian clergy. *Shízì Sì* (十字寺) = "Cross Temple," the common name for Nestorian churches. |
| | *guān* (Daoist) / *miào* | 觀 / 廟 | Daoism / Chinese folk | *Guān* = Daoist temple. The Quánzhēn (全真) Daoist school flourished under early Mongol patronage before losing favor to Buddhists in debates. |
| | *qīngzhēn sì* / *lǐbài sì* | 清真寺 / 禮拜寺 | Islam | Mosques existed in Yuan China, particularly in Dadu (Beijing), Quanzhou, and other trade cities with significant Muslim populations (mostly Central Asian *sèmù rén* 色目人). |
| **India** (Delhi) | *masjid* / *masjid-i jāmi'* | مسجد | Islam | The *Quwwat al-Islām* (قوة الإسلام, "Might of Islam") mosque in Delhi and the *Arhai Din ka Jhonpra* in Ajmer are famous early Sultanate congregational mosques. Many were built using spolia from demolished Hindu/Jain temples. |
| | *mandir* / *devalaya* (Skt.) | मन्दिर / देवालय | Hinduism | *Mandir* = temple. Muslim chroniclers also used *but-khāna* ("idol-house"). While many temples were destroyed during conquests, Hindu worship continued in many areas. The coastal kingdoms (e.g., in Gujarat, Malabar) maintained active temple traditions. |
| | *vihāra* | विहार | Buddhism (residual) | Buddhism was in steep decline in mainland India by the 13th c. The last major Buddhist center, Nālandā, was sacked c. 1200. *Vihāra* was still used as a term in historical memory. |
| **Maritime Asia** | *candi* | (Old Javanese) | Hindu-Buddhist | *Candi* was the universal term for temple/shrine in Java — both Hindu and Buddhist. Majapahit architecture produced the *candi bentar* (split gate) and monumental temples like Candi Panataran. |
| | *prāsāda* | (Sanskrit/OJ) | Hindu-Buddhist | *Prāsāda* = tower-sanctuary, the tall central shrine housing the deity image. |
| | *masjid* / *langgar* | مسجد / (Malay) | Islam | *Masjid* appeared with Islam in Pasai (Sumatra, c. 1267). *Langgar* = small neighborhood prayer house in Malay/Javanese usage. By the late 13th c., Samudera Pasai was the first Islamic sultanate in the archipelago; tombstones with Arabic inscriptions date to 1297. |

## 5. Market / Bazaar

| Region | Term (Transliteration) | Original Script | Language | Historical Note |
|--------|----------------------|-----------------|----------|-----------------|
| **West Asia** | *bāzār* / *sūq* / *qaysariyya* / *khān* | بازار / سوق / قيصارية / خان | Persian/Arabic | *Bāzār* (Persian) = the primary term for marketplace. *Sūq* (Arabic) was used interchangeably. The *bāzār* typically formed a linear market street near the Friday mosque, with side lanes (*rāsta*) for specialized trades. *Qaysariyya* = covered market hall for luxury goods. *Khān* = urban caravanserai combining warehouse, lodging, and commerce. Under Ghazan Khan, Tabriz's covered bāzār spanned 29 acres — the largest in the region. |
| **Central Asia** | *bāzār* / *tīm* / *chār-sū* | بازار / تيم / چارسو | Persian/Turkic | *Bāzār* was standard. *Tīm* = a domed market building (later a Timurid specialty). *Chār-sū* = crossroads market at the intersection of two main bāzār streets. Samarkand's *Registan* area already functioned as a commercial hub. |
| **Steppe** (Mongol) | *bāzār* / *zhèn* (Chinese context) | بازار / 鎮 | Persian/Chinese | Qaraqorum, the Mongol capital, had a walled commercial quarter with foreign merchant communities and a bāzār. William of Rubruck (1254) described its markets. In Yuan China, markets used Chinese terminology: *shì* (市) or *zhèn* (鎮). |
| **India** (Delhi) | *bāzār* / *mandī* / *sarā-i 'adl* / *chowk* | بازار / مندى / سراى عدل / चौक | Persian/Hindi | *Bāzār* (Persian) was the standard term. *Mandī* = central grain market. Alauddin Khalji (r. 1296-1316) established the *Sarā-i 'Adl* ("Mart of Justice") in Delhi, a regulated state market for manufactured and imported goods with fixed prices. *Chowk* (Hindi) = a marketplace square at a crossroads. |
| **Maritime Asia** | *pken* / *pasar* | (Old Javanese) / ڤاسر (Jawi) | Old Javanese/Malay | *Pken* (Old Javanese) = the local market, held on specific days of the *pancawara* (five-day market week). *Pasar* (Malay, from Persian *bāzār*) entered the archipelago via trade. Srivijaya's ports hosted bustling markets for Chinese, Indian, and Arab merchants. |

## 6. Currency Unit

| Region | Term (Transliteration) | Original Script | Metal/Type | Historical Note |
|--------|----------------------|-----------------|------------|-----------------|
| **West Asia** | *dirham* / *dīnār* | درهم / دينار | Silver / Gold | The *dirham* was the standard Ilkhanid silver coin. Coins were minted in Tabriz, Baghdad, Shiraz, and other cities, often bearing Arabic, Persian, and Uyghur inscriptions — a unique synthesis of Mongol and Islamic numismatic traditions. The gold *dīnār* was less common but used for large transactions and hoarding. |
| **Central Asia** | *dirham* / *dīnār* | درهم / دينار | Silver / Gold | Samarkand and Bukhara continued pre-Mongol minting traditions. Kebek Khan's reforms (1307-1326) introduced a new standardized silver coinage. *Tamghā* tax was often paid in coin rather than in kind. |
| **Steppe** (Mongol) | *zhōngtǒng chāo* (paper) / *dìng* (silver ingot) / *yastuq* (silver) | 中統鈔 / 錠 / yastuq | Paper / Silver | The Yuan dynasty (1271-1368) was the first to make paper money the sole legal tender. *Zhōngtǒng chāo* (中統鈔) = paper notes pegged to silver, issued from 1260. *Dìng* (錠) = a silver sycee/ingot of ~50 *liǎng* (taels, ~1.85 kg), used as a store of value. *Yastuq* = the Mongol term for a silver ingot (also called *süke*). 1 dìng = 50 liǎng = 100 guàn of paper money. |
| **India** (Delhi) | *tanka* / *jītal* | टंका / जीतल | Silver / Copper | The silver *tanka* (c. 175 grains/11.3 g) was introduced by Sultan Iltutmish (r. 1211-1236) as the standard large-transaction coin. The copper *jītal* was the fractional coin for daily trade, with 1 tanka ≈ 48-50 jitals. Gold tankas were introduced by Balban (r. 1266-1287). Alauddin Khalji regulated prices in jitals: wheat at 7.5 jitals per *mann* (~11.5 kg). |
| **Maritime Asia** | *māsa* / *tahil* / *kupang* / *picis* | (Old Javanese) | Gold/Silver/Copper | Pre-Majapahit Java (9th-13th c.) used a weight-based system: 1 *kati* (~609 g) = 16 *tahil* (tael, ~38.4 g) = 256 *māsa* (2.4 g) = 1,024 *kupang* (0.6 g). Silver "sandalwood flower" coins circulated. By ~1300, Chinese copper cash (*picis* / *kepeng*, from *pitis*) displaced precious metal coins. Sumatra (Pasai) minted gold *mas* coins with Arabic inscriptions. |

## 7. Ruler / Governor / Lord (替代"皇上"/"府尹")

| Region | Term (Transliteration) | Original Script | Language | Historical Note |
|--------|----------------------|-----------------|----------|-----------------|
| **West Asia** | *īlkhān* (ruler) / *pādishāh* / *amīr* / *wālī* / *shahna* / *malik* / *sulṭān* | ایلخان / پادشاه / امیر / والی / شحنه / ملک / سلطان | Mongol/Persian/Arabic | *Īlkhān* = the dynastic title of Hülegü's line, meaning "subordinate khan" (to the Great Khan) or "khan of the il (people/nation)." *Pādishāh* = sovereign (Persian, equivalent to "emperor"). *Amīr* = military commander/noble. *Wālī* = provincial governor. *Shahna* = military overseer/military governor of a city or province. *Malik* = king/lord (Arabic). *Sulṭān* = authority, used increasingly after Ghazan's conversion to Islam. Ghazan's coins read "Ghāzān, Emperor of Islam." |
| **Central Asia** | *khān* / *amīr* / *malik* | خان / امیر / ملک | Mongol/Persian/Arabic | *Khān* = the supreme title of the Chagatai ruler. *Amīr* = tribal military leader — in the fragmented Chagatai Khanate, amirs often held real power. *Malik* = lesser king or regional lord. |
| **Steppe** (Mongol) | *khāghān / qa'an* (Great Khan) / *khān* / *noyan* / *tayiji* | ᠬᠠᠭᠠᠨ / ᠬᠠᠨ / ᠨᠣᠶᠠᠨ | Mongolian | *Khāghān* (qa'an) = the Great Khan, supreme ruler of the Mongol Empire. *Khān* = ruler of a khanate or ulus. *Noyan* = lord/commander, a hereditary noble title. *Tayiji* = prince (from Chinese *tàizǐ* 太子). |
| **India** (Delhi) | *sulṭān* / *malik* / *khān* / *amīr* / *muqtī* / *wālī* / *nāzim* | سلطان / ملک / خان / امیر / مقطع / والی / ناظم | Arabic/Persian | *Sulṭān* = the supreme ruler. *Malik* = king/lord (rank in nobility). *Khān* = the highest noble rank below the Sultan (originally commander of 100,000). *Amīr* = noble/commander. *Muqtī* / *iqtadār* = the holder of an *iqta'* (provincial revenue assignment) — effectively the provincial governor. *Wālī* = governor of a major province. *Nāzim* = administrator. |
| **Maritime Asia** | *rāja* / *mahārāja* / *bhre* / *sulṭān* / *dātu* | (Old Javanese) / سلطان | Sanskrit/OJ/Malay/Arabic | In Hindu-Buddhist Java: *Rāja* = king, *mahārāja* = great king, *śrī mahārāja* = royal title. *Bhre* = title for Majapahit princes governing provinces (e.g., *Bhre Daha* = Prince of Daha). *Dātu* (Malay) = chief. In Islamic Pasai (Sumatra): *Sulṭān* (from Arabic) was adopted by the first Muslim ruler, Sultan Malik al-Saleh (d. 1297). |

## 8. Post House / Way Station (替代"驿站")

| Region | Term (Transliteration) | Original Script | Language | Historical Note |
|--------|----------------------|-----------------|----------|-----------------|
| **West Asia** | *yām* / *yām-khāna* / *ribāt* / *kārvān-sarā* | یام / یامخانه / رباط / کاروانسرا | Mongol/Persian | The *yām* (Mongol postal relay) was adopted throughout the Ilkhanate. Stations were spaced 18-30 km apart, providing fresh horses and supplies for official couriers (*yamchi*). *Ribāt* = a fortified way station (originally a frontier fort, later an inn). *Kārvān-sarā* = caravanserai for commercial travelers. Ghazan Khan reformed the yām system to curb abuses. |
| **Central Asia** | *yām* / *jam* / *yamhāne* / *ribāt-i yamhāne* | یام / جام / یمخانه / رباط یمخانه | Turkic/Persian | The Chagatai Khanate maintained the yām relay system. Old Uyghur documents from Turfan detail the operation of postal stations, including provisions of horses, fodder, and food for couriers (*ula'achi*). *Yamhāne* (Turkish/Persian hybrid) = post station building. |
| **Steppe** (Mongol) | *yām* / *jam* / *örtöö* / *ula'a* | ᠥᠷᠲᠥᠥ / ᠥᠯᠠᠭᠠ (Mongol script) | Mongolian | *Yām* / *jam* = the postal relay system, one of the Mongol Empire's greatest institutions, established empire-wide by Ögedei Khan (r. 1229-1241). *Örtöö* (lit. "checkpoint") = a relay station, typically 20-40 miles apart. *Ula'a* = courier service, also the obligation to provide relay horses. Staff included *yamchi* (post station staff) and *ula'achi* (couriers). |
| **India** (Delhi) | *barīd* / *dāk chowkī* / *ulāgh* | بريد / डाक चौकी | Persian/Hindustani | *Barīd* = the state postal and intelligence system inherited from the Abbasid tradition. The *barīd-i mamālik* was the head of posts/intelligence. *Dāk chowkī* = relay post station (later term). *Ulāgh* (from Mongol *ula'a*) = the courier/mount system — the Delhi sultans adapted the Mongol yām for military communication. |
| **Maritime Asia** | (no formal relay system) | — | — | The Hindu-Buddhist and early Islamic polities of maritime SE Asia did not maintain a formal land-based postal relay network comparable to the Mongol *yām*. Communication relied on royal envoys (*caraka* in Old Javanese) and messengers traveling by river and sea. Coastal way stations at ports served as rest points for maritime travelers. |

## 9. City Gate (替代"城门")

| Region | Term (Transliteration) | Original Script | Language | Historical Note |
|--------|----------------------|-----------------|----------|-----------------|
| **West Asia** | *darvāza* / *bāb* | دروازه / باب | Persian/Arabic | *Darvāza* (Persian) = gate. Ilkhanid cities had multiple named gates (e.g., Tabriz had the Mehrījerd Gate). *Bāb* (Arabic) was used in Arabic-speaking regions like Baghdad and Damascus. Gates were collection points for the *tamghā* tax and sometimes had attached customs offices. |
| **Central Asia** | *darvāza* | دروازه | Persian/Turkic | Samarkand had six gates, Bukhara had seven (later twelve). Gates were named for their destinations: *Darvāza-i Samarqand* (Samarkand Gate in Bukhara), *Darvāza-i Bukhara* (Bukhara Gate in Samarkand). Gates were defensible structures of mud-brick or fired brick. |
| **Steppe** (Mongol) | *qaghalgha* (Mongol) / *chéng mén* (Chinese) | ᠬᠠᠭᠠᠯᠭᠠ / 城門 | Mongolian / Chinese | *Qaghalgha* = gate in Mongolian. Qaraqorum had a walled perimeter with gates. In the Yuan context (Dadu/Khanbaliq), *chéng mén* (城門) was the Chinese term. Dadu had 11 city gates, each named (e.g., Jiànmén 健門). |
| **India** (Delhi) | *darwāza* / *dvār* | دروازه / द्वार | Persian/Hindi | *Darwāza* (from Persian) was the standard term. Delhi's city gates included the *Badā'ūn Darwāza* and *Ghaznī Darwāza*. *Dvār* (Sanskrit/Hindi) was used in non-Persianate contexts. The gates of Tughluqabad (early 14th c.) feature massive fortified corridors with guard rooms. |
| **Maritime Asia** | *gapura* / *candi bentar* / *paduraksa* / *wiwara* | (Old Javanese) | Old Javanese | *Gapura* = gate/entrance to a temple or palace compound. *Candi bentar* = the iconic split gate (symmetrical halves framing a passage, no doors) — a Majapahit architectural innovation. *Paduraksa* = a roofed gateway with an enclosed door. *Wiwara* = door/opening, often used in sacred/ritual context. The Wringin Lawang *candi bentar* at Trowulan survives from the Majapahit period. |

## 10. Quarter / District / Ward

| Region | Term (Transliteration) | Original Script | Language | Historical Note |
|--------|----------------------|-----------------|----------|-----------------|
| **West Asia** | *mahalla* / *rabad* / *shahristān* / *kūy* / *rāsta* | محله / ربض / شهرستان / کوی / راسته | Arabic/Persian | *Mahalla* = residential quarter (from Arabic *mahalla*). *Rabaḍ* = the outer suburb beyond the city walls, where craftsmen and markets were located. *Shahristān* = the inner walled city core. *Kūy* (Persian) = lane/neighborhood. *Rāsta* = a market quarter or street of shops in the bāzār (e.g., *Rāsta-ye Bāzār-e Jadīd* in Tabriz). |
| **Central Asia** | *mahalla* / *guzar* / *rabad* / *shahristān* | محله / گذر / ربض / شهرستان | Persian/Arabic/Turkic | *Mahalla* and *guzar* (Persian "passage/neighborhood") were the key terms. A *guzar* centered on a neighborhood mosque, school, and water cistern. Samarkand was divided into quarters (*daha*), each containing ~30 guzars. Trades were grouped: "clean trades" (jewelry, textiles) inside the *shahristān*, "dirty trades" (pottery, smithing) in the *rabad*. |
| **Steppe** (Mongol) | *ayimag* / *ordu* | ᠠᠶᠢᠮᠠᠭ / ᠣᠷᠳᠤ (Mongol script) | Mongolian | *Ayimag* = tribal division/group, sometimes a territorial unit. *Ordu* = the khan's camp/court/palace complex — the mobile center of authority. In Qaraqorum, the city had distinct quarters for different ethnic groups (Chinese artificers, Muslim merchants, Nestorians, Buddhists) as described by William of Rubruck. |
| **India** (Delhi) | *mahalla* / *mohallā* | محله / मोहल्ला | Persian/Hindi | *Mahalla* (Persian) / *mohallā* (Hindi) = residential quarter, often organized by profession, ethnicity, or religion. Medieval Delhi had quarters for different craft guilds, foreign merchants, and religious communities. Ibn Battuta (14th c.) described Delhi's specialized quarters. *Urdu-mu'allā* = the mobile "camp city" that traveled with the Sultan's court. |
| **Maritime Asia** | *kampung* / *kabuyutan* / *tpi siring* | (Malay/OJ) | Malay, Old Javanese | *Kampung* (Malay) = village/quarter, the basic residential unit. Foreign merchants were often assigned specific quarters in port cities. *Kabuyutan* (Old Javanese) = sacred/hermitage district. *Tpi siring* = suburban settlement (literally "edge/bank settlement"). Srivijaya and Majapahit port cities had distinct quarters for Chinese, Arab, Indian, and local traders. |

## Sources & Methodology

This table was compiled through systematic web search of academic sources, cross-referencing:

- **Encyclopaedia Iranica** (iranicaonline.org) — authoritative reference for Ilkhanid and Chagatai-era terminology, customs duties, taxation, architecture, urbanism
- **Cambridge History of the Mongol Empire** — Ilkhanate administration, titles
- **The Secret History of the Mongols** — original Mongol terminology for postal system, titles
- **Old Uyghur Documents Concerning the Postal System of the Mongol Empire** (Berliner Turfantexte) — Chagatai-era yām terminology
- **Tarīkh-i Fīrūz Shāhī** (Barani) and **Tabaqāt-i Nāsirī** (Jūzjānī) — Delhi Sultanate administration
- **Nāgara-Kērtāgama** (1365) — Majapahit terminology
- **Pararaton** and Old Javanese inscriptions — Singhasari/Majapahit titles
- **Nawanatya** — Majapahit court organization
- **Yuán Shǐ** (元史) and Zhìshùn Zhènjiāng Zhì (至順鎮江志) — Yuan dynasty religious and postal terminology
- Numismatic sources: Wicks (1986), Christie (1996) — Javanese currency systems
- **Al-Murshid fī al-Ḥisāb** (Qāshānī) — Ilkhanid fiscal administration

Where a term was uncertain or only attested in a later period, this is noted. All transliterations use simplified scholarly romanization. Scripts provided are the historically attested forms for the 13th century (not modern orthographies).
