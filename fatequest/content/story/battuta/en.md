## city.maldive.name

The Maldives

## ev.maldive.entry.title

The Coconut Isles

## ev.maldive.entry.body

These isles are a wonder of the world — some two thousand of them, near a hundred
ringed so close together that the people of one isle know the people of the next.
The cocoa-nut palm is the greatest tree, bearing twelve crops in the year, so that
fruit green, ripe, and dry hang together on the same bough; from the nut men make
palm-wine, oil, and a strong thread with which the ships of India and Yemen are
sewn, yielding against a rock where iron nails would break. The coin of the land is
the wada, a sea-shell buried in the earth till the flesh wastes away. A woman, the
Queen Khodīja, rules the chief isle; the women keep their own company at table and
never leave their districts; and the judge's word is obeyed like a king's.

toward guests: the woman in whose house you lodge will cook for you and attend you
for a very small consideration, and the wada shells pay your way among the islands.

## ev.maldive.battuta_a.title

Judge, Three Wives, and Flight

## ev.maldive.battuta_a.body

The vizier Jamāl Oddīn, husband of the Queen, receives you with honour: a house with
a garden, a dress of honour, and a horse, though none but the vizier rides, the
nobles going in palanquins or afoot. He asks you to take the office of judge, whose
word is obeyed like a king's, and whose right is the revenue of three islands. You
accept. Under the island custom a traveller may marry for a very small dowry, on
condition of divorce at his departure, for the women never leave their districts; so
you marry three wives. Your kin become numerous and powerful, and the vizier, weak
in troops and unused to war, begins to fear you, inquiring into your affairs and
watching your steps in secret. You take thought to leave the islands.

## ev.alexandria.battuta_a.title

A Judge Named at the Gate

## ev.alexandria.battuta_a.body

At evening the keeper of Alexandria's gate is impatient to close it, for a poor
scholar sits near the gate and will not enter until he has seen some favourable
omen. He came from the Hejaz poor, and as all the travellers go in and it is nearly
time to close, the keeper cries to him in irony: Enter, Mr Judge. He answers: Yes,
judge, if that be God's will. He enters one of the colleges, and attends to reading
so diligently that his name and modesty reach the king of Egypt. When the judge of
Alexandria dies, many learned men of the city expect the appointment — the scholar
expects nothing, and the sultan sends it to him.

## ev.alexandria.battuta_b.title

The Saint's Prophecy of Three Brothers

## ev.alexandria.battuta_b.body

One of the greatest saints of Alexandria is the learned and pious Imam Borhāna
Oddīn El Aaraj, a worker of miracles. You go in to him, and he says: I perceive that
you are fond of travelling into various countries. You say yes, though you have no
intention of travelling into very distant parts. He replies: You must visit my
brother Farīd Oddīn in India, my brother Rokn Oddīn Ibn Zakaryā in Sind, and my
brother Borhān Oddīn in China, and when you see them, present my compliments to
them. You are astonished at his words, and determine to visit those countries; nor
do you give up the purpose till you have met all the three and presented his
compliments.

## ev.accon.battuta_a.title

A Traveller's Pomegranate Story

## ev.accon.battuta_a.body

Under a piece of shade cloth beside the sea at Acre, Hamid is rolling up an old mat. He says he has come from Kark Nuh inland, where he once stayed a night at a lodge. Adil, who sells water to travellers, fills his cup and asks him to finish his tale. His daughter Safa sits nearby.

“People there tell stories about Abu Yaakub Yusuf,” Hamid says. “They say he was once a king, then made his living weaving mats. They also say he kept an orchard in Damascus for Nur al-Din.”

Safa asks whether the pomegranates were sweet.

“The ones he brought were sour, so the story goes,” Hamid says. “When someone asked why he hadn't chosen sweet ones, he said he had been hired to guard the orchard and had never eaten its fruit.”

Safa puts a pomegranate on a wooden board and asks Hamid to guess whether it is sweet. He weighs it in his hand and puts it down again. “When we borrow a knife, we can all try some.” You listen as they discuss whom to ask.

## ev.ev_accon_battuta_a.choice_1_result

You spend a day with Hamid at the resting places along the shore, helping him find a companion who knows the hill roads. At Kark Nuh, he says, travellers can drink at the lodge, and someone will show them where the tomb stands. It lies inland, and he hopes to go with someone who knows the way.

Back beneath the shade cloth, Safa has cut open the pomegranate. She offers Hamid a piece. He tastes it and laughs that she chose a good one. Adil gives you a piece too and thanks you for helping Hamid find a companion. The travellers who rest at Acre come to know you.

## ev.ev_accon_battuta_a.choice_2_result

You walk with Hamid along the old harbour steps. He points north along the coast and describes the road towards Tyre. He once watched people mending boat nets there and borrowed a small knife from one of them.

You spend the day asking people along the shore. At dusk a netmender lends you a small knife. When Safa hears, she brings out the pomegranate at once. You mark the direction of Tyre on your map.

## ev.ev_accon_battuta_a.choice_3_result

Hamid sets the mat aside. Travellers, he says, tell another story about Abu Yaakub Yusuf: that he once turned the copper vessels of a young woman's dowry into gold. Safa stares and asks whether Hamid saw it himself.

“I saw the lodge and his tomb,” Hamid says. He tells you how he lost the road inland until a man with a donkey led him to the door. You listen through the day and record the marvel as a story alongside the road Hamid actually travelled. At dusk, Adil borrows a knife and Safa cuts the pomegranate for everyone. Then he invites you to tell the story to newly arrived travellers, who are glad to listen.

## ev.yasdi.battuta_a.title

A Lodging Keeper’s Tale

## ev.yasdi.battuta_a.body

In a Yazd lodging house, the keeper, Hamid, is mending a torn mat. His daughter brings bread to two travellers who came from Isfahan and had earlier crossed the Fars hills. They tell her how small lodgings along that mountain road keep food ready for people who arrive late.

‘Do they know who is coming?’ she asks. ‘Usually not,’ one traveller says. ‘That’s why your father keeps an extra loaf, isn’t it?’ Hamid smiles and asks her to set the bread beside the guests. The travellers also pass on a story they heard in Shiraz about a religious elder who welcomed strangers. You can stay overnight, join the evening prayers, or ask them about the road they took from Isfahan to Yazd.

## ev.bochara.battuta_a.title

The Old School Wall in Bukhara

## ev.bochara.battuta_a.body

Near an old school wall in Bukhara, a melon seller is helping her father patch the shade over his stall. Some of the nearby buildings were damaged in earlier wars and rebuilt in stages; this wall remains, and local children use it to measure how tall they have grown. Her father recalls a family story of these lanes after an earlier war, when some houses needed rebuilding. The seller asks whether this wall stood then too. He says older neighbours remember it standing.

An older neighbour joins them with a story he heard as a boy: the conqueror, he says, began as a blacksmith in Khotan. The melon seller interrupts. ‘My father heard another version. Tell it as a story, not as his life.’ The neighbour laughs and admits he cannot name the first teller. He does know the caravan road to Samarkand; he has sent fruit that way.

You ask about the wall, the road, and the melon slices drying beneath the shade. The seller offers to show you where the old school stood, the neighbour can spend the day tracing the Samarkand road with you, or you can pay a little for a piece of melon and eat it while they finish the roof.

## ev.delli.battuta_a.title

The Courtyard Register

## ev.delli.battuta_a.body

At a grain courtyard in Delhi, Shirin has laid a damp register beneath a cloth weight. Rain blurred several household marks before the morning distribution. Her daughter Safiya brings an empty sack and asks, ‘Did Uncle Rahim get his share?’ Shirin holds the page toward the light. ‘I can see his name. I cannot tell whether I wrote one measure or two.’ The old clerk at the next table says he still has the wooden tally from yesterday, but his hands are busy weighing the next family’s grain.

You ask why everyone is waiting in the yard. ‘If I guess at a number, somebody will go home short,’ Shirin says. Safiya offers to carry the register to her uncle; her mother stops her because the wet ink may smear again. A trader waiting with the other families says his pack animals leave for Cambay after noon. He has used these grain yards on the westward road and can name the places where travellers find water.

Shirin asks you to hold down the page while she brings the tally over. Safiya points to the mark beside her uncle’s name, and the clerk squints at it before he answers.

## ev.delli.battuta_b.title

The Two Road Bundles

## ev.delli.battuta_b.body

At a travellers’ house in Delhi, keeper Bibi Hina is sorting two bundles of letters before their carriers leave. Her nephew marked one bundle for the westward caravan and the other for the long eastern road, then set both beneath a leaking roof tile. ‘I know which cloth I used,’ he says, ‘but the ink on the tags has run.’ Hina has him open the outer wraps and read the names aloud before anyone takes a letter away.

You ask who will carry the westward bundle. A driver bound toward Cambay raises his hand from the doorway; his sister lives near a stopping place on that road. The eastern carrier says the route toward Khotan takes many changes of hands, so each address must be clear. Hina asks whether he has enough dry cloth to rewrap it. He shows her a single piece and admits he had saved the rest for his own bedding. She sends her nephew to fetch another piece from the storeroom.

A family has arrived looking for a room, and Hina must settle them before dark. She asks you to hold the letters in order while her nephew brings back the dry wrapping.

## ev.melibar.battuta_a.title

The Chinese Junks and the Wreck in the Night

## ev.melibar.battuta_a.body

You reach Kalikut, one of the great ports of Malabar, and wait three months for the
season when the sea of China is navigable. In the harbour ride the great junks, each
like an independent city: six hundred sailors and four hundred soldiers aboard the
largest, with houses of wood built on deck, and oars like masts, twenty-five men
working standing. They are built nowhere but at El Zaitun and Sin Kilan. One night
the sea rises; the junk bearing the Emperor's present strikes the shore, and all on
board perish with the wealth. You have stayed on land for the Friday prayer, and are
saved.

with your servants and everything you owned.

## ev.cail.battuta_a.title

Pilgrims at the Cail Landing

## ev.cail.battuta_a.body

At Cail's landing, a family is packing for a crossing toward the island where pilgrims speak of Adam's Peak. A young woman has tied the water skins too tightly to the same pole as her mother's bedding. “If you leave them there, I will have to lift your whole bed whenever I want a drink,” her mother says. The daughter loosens the knot and asks an older pilgrim which road people take after landing. He says travellers tell different stories about the mountain and its sacred footprint; he has not yet seen it himself. “Then tell us what you do know,” the mother says. He points out where their boat will take on water and which people in the harbour know the crossing from Maabar. A gem trader listening nearby opens his box and offers you a ruby brought by overland merchants. The boatman says the water and food will take three days to load. The daughter closes her pack and asks you to help her check the provisions before departure.

## ev.java-major.battuta_a.title

The Warlike Queen of Tawalisi

## ev.java-major.battuta_a.body

The people of Tawalisi are handsome and strong; their land, named after its king, is
so great that he will oppose the Emperor of China with his junks. Their women ride
on horseback, excel in throwing the javelin, and fight like men in battle. In the
port of Kailuka the governor is the king's daughter: she sits on the throne of
government, her women before her with the papers of state, greets you in Turkish,
and writes the Bismilla with her own hand. She wonders at the wealth of India and
says she must conquer it for herself. She keeps a regiment of women in her army, and
no suitor has dared to overcome her in the tournament.

## ev.zayton.battuta_a.title

Paper Money, Painters, and the Registry of Ships

## ev.zayton.battuta_a.body

The traveller's tale is borne out as you walk the quay: in China you buy and sell
with paper. A palm-sized sheet, stamped with the king's stamp, is money:
twenty-five such notes make a nashhat, a dinar of the place, and torn notes are
exchanged at the mint for new ones, without interest. No one will take a dinar or
a dirhem until it is changed for these notes. The painters are the most skilful
in the world: every stranger who passes through their towns finds his likeness
upon the walls and on papers stuck up in the streets. When a vessel leaves China,
the names and forms of the men aboard are written down, and when it returns the
magistrates' servants board it and compare; if a man is missing, the commander of
the vessel is seized, and goods not entered in the register forfeit ship and cargo
to the king — an oppression seen nowhere else.

## ev.ev_zayton_battuta_a.choice_1_result

You take your dinars and dirhems to the changers, for no one in China will deal with
you until your coin is changed into the king's notes. Each note is a palm-sized

## ev.ev_zayton_battuta_a.choice_2_result

custom with every stranger who passes through their towns: should any such a one
make flight necessary, his picture is sent out to the other provinces, and
wherever he is, he is taken.

## ev.ev_zayton_battuta_a.choice_3_result

You spend a day watching the clerks of the harbour take down the names and forms
of the men aboard each junk before it sails, and when a vessel returns, the
servants of the
magistrates board her and compare the men with the descriptions; a missing man
costs the commander his ship, and goods not entered in the register forfeit ship
and cargo to the king — an oppression seen nowhere else. You learn of the river
road, twenty-seven days from Sin Kilan, where earthenware is made as here at
Zayton, and that the same river runs on toward Kinsay; here the river called the
water of life enters the sea, and the place is called the meeting of two seas.

The King's Paper Money

## codex.cx-paper-money.body

In China the king's paper is money: palm-sized sheets stamped with his stamp,
twenty-five to a nashhat. Torn notes are exchanged at the mint for new ones without
interest, and no one will take a dinar or a dirhem until it is changed for these
notes.

## ev.kinsay.battuta_a.title

The Homeward Voyage to the West

## ev.kinsay.battuta_a.body

You go down the river from El Khansa, past Kanjanfur, to El Zaitun, where you find
junks bound for India, and take passage in a junk of El Malik El Zahir, the King of
Sumatra, whose servants are Muslims. For ten days a good wind holds; then the sky
grows dark, and a storm drives the junk into a sea unknown to the sailors. At
daybreak a mountain stands twenty miles away, and the sailors take leave of one
another, saying it is a Rokh, and if he sees us we are lost. You betake yourselves
to prayer, and God gives a good wind; after two months you reach Java, and shortly
after, Sumatra.

## ev.ev_kinsay_battuta_a.choice_1_result

You find junks bound for India in the port of El Zaitun and take passage in one
belonging to El Malik El Zahir, the King of Sumatra, whose servants are Muslims.
You go down the river from El Khansa, past Kanjanfur, to the sea, and set your
course with a good wind for the open ocean and the long road home.

## ev.ev_kinsay_battuta_a.choice_2_result

The sky grows dark and the storm drives the junk into a sea unknown to the sailors;
they are terribly afraid and wish to put back, but it is impossible. You betake
yourselves to repentance and prayer with all your hearts, and the merchants make
many vows. After sunrise the wind calms in some degree, and God gives you a good
wind to steer your course away from peril.

## ev.ev_kinsay_battuta_a.choice_3_result

At daybreak the sailors point to a mountain in the sea, about twenty miles away,
though you are far from land and no mountain was ever seen in that part of the sea.
They look upon it with the utmost perturbation and take leave of one another, saying
it is a Rokh, and that if he sees you, you shall assuredly perish. But a good wind
comes, and you steer away, and see no more of him, nor ever know the particulars of
his shape.
