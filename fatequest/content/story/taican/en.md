## city.taican.name

Taican

## ev.taican.entry.body

At the entrance to Taican’s grain market, a porter named Salim is steadying a cart while his daughter ties a cloth over the top sacks. She asks him to lift one corner so she can see the family mark. ‘Your aunt’s grain goes to the mill,’ he says. ‘Ours stays for the road.’ She finds the mark and calls to the miller waiting by the gate.

You ask whether travellers can buy supplies here. Salim points to the scales and says the merchants will post their measures after the morning weighing. His daughter knows which sacks belong to the neighbours who worship at the local mosque; she is taking their small share over before they close the yard. The miller offers to walk with you through the market tomorrow if you want to stay.

The cart wheel catches in a rut. Salim and his daughter put their shoulders to it, while the miller moves the loose stones. Once the wheel rolls free, Salim checks the two family marks again before letting the cart go.

## ev.taican.a.title

The Grain Master’s Two Tallies

## ev.taican.a.body

At the corn market, grain master Hamza has written two tallies for the same cart. His apprentice says one belongs to the miller and one to a caravan bound toward Badashan. The marks on the sacks are faint, so Hamza asks the carter to name each family before he weighs the load. ‘My sister will be here soon,’ the carter says. ‘She tied them. She will know.’

You ask why he cannot simply untie the sacks. Hamza says he can, but the families agreed to keep them sealed until the grain is weighed. The sister arrives with a strip of blue thread and puts it beside the cart’s rear wheel. The caravan sacks were tied with blue; the miller’s were not. The apprentice checks each knot and writes the names in the right columns.

Hamza’s wife has a full bale of cotton cloth for 108 silver. Hamza invites you to watch the weighing for a day, or to ask the caravan masters about Balc and Badashan once they have collected their grain.

## ev.taican.b.title

At the Salt-Cutting Yard

## ev.taican.b.body

At a yard below the salt hills, miner Farid is checking the head of his iron pick. His sister brings a broken block to the scale and complains that it looks larger than its weight. Farid turns it over: a hollow had been hidden underneath. ‘Tell the buyers the weight,’ she says. ‘They can see the shape for themselves.’

You ask how the salt comes down from the hills. Farid shows you a path used by the pack animals and says the cutters work slowly when the rock is hard. His sister has promised their mother to bring home enough for winter preserving. A neighbour arrives to ask whether a caravan going toward Sapurgan has room for one more load. Farid says the driver will return after supper and can answer.

A full trading block of salt costs 108 silver. Farid also invites you to watch the cutters for two days if you want to see how they prepare the loads. His sister takes the weighed block aside and writes its measure where the buyer can see it.

## ev.ev_taican_a_followup.title

Hamza’s Road Book

## ev.ev_taican_a_followup.body

Hamza opens his road book beside the grain scales. His apprentice has copied the Blue Thread cart into it but put Badashan on the miller’s line. The carter’s sister returns and points to the correct column without scolding him. ‘We checked the knots together,’ she says. ‘Write what we actually saw.’ The apprentice erases the place name and copies it again.

You ask what else Hamza records. He keeps notes on which caravans carry grain toward Balc and Badashan, with dates and loads. His wife has another full bale of cotton cloth for 108 silver, separate from the one offered earlier. A buyer wants to wait for tomorrow’s posted grain price before choosing his load. Hamza gives him a place on the bench and asks the apprentice to bring water.

The carter’s sister checks the corrected line. She smiles when the apprentice finally has the blue thread in the right column, then asks whether you need the two roads marked on your map.

## ev.taican.sackmark.title

The Sack with No Blue Thread

## ev.taican.sackmark.body

In a yard behind the grain market, a widow named Layla is dividing her harvest between her household and her daughter’s new home. The sacks are the same size. Her daughter marks one with blue thread, but the last piece breaks in her hand. Layla asks her to use a knot in the rope instead. ‘Your grandfather taught me that when thread ran short,’ she says.

You ask how the daughter will recognise her share after the cart is loaded. She shows you the knot, then admits she has never tied it tightly enough on a full sack. Layla calls her younger son to hold the mouth of the bag while she teaches the knot again. The boy counts the turns out loud and gets ahead of her. The daughter laughs and starts over.

The cart driver is ready to leave. He says he can pay a helper 0.8 silver to carry the marked sack to the daughter’s house today. Layla would also welcome someone who stays a day to teach the children how to check the marks after the sacks are weighed. She puts the broken thread in her pocket and watches the daughter finish the knot.

## ev.taican.sackmark.choice.deliver

Deliver the marked sack today

## ev.taican.sackmark.result.deliver

You carry the marked sack to Layla’s daughter’s house. She unties the knot in front of her husband and shows him how to make it again. The driver pays you 0.8 silver for the delivery.

## ev.taican.sackmark.choice.stay

Stay a day and help the children check the sacks

## ev.taican.sackmark.result.stay

You spend the day with Layla and the children, comparing the knots after the sacks leave the scales. By supper the daughter and her brother can each find the right load. Layla thanks you for helping them keep both households’ shares clear.
