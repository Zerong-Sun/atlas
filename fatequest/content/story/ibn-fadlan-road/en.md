## ev.road.fadlan_01.title

The Steppe Ice Crossing

## ev.road.fadlan_01.body

The caravan reaches a frozen river on the steppe. An oil merchant lifts the flap of his cart and checks the jars packed in straw. His sister is waiting for the load across the river. An older traveller recalls a passage from Ibn Fadlan's account: at the Jayhun, the ice was said to be seventeen spans thick, and laden carts could cross it. Here, a cart is already halfway over, its wheels pressing two tracks through the snow.

The merchant asks the keeper of the waystation whether he should follow it. The keeper crouches by the bank and knocks the ice with the butt of his staff. “I sent two men to look at the upper crossing,” he says. “They'll be back before supper. If you're going now, keep to the cart tracks.” The merchant calls his hired driver over and prepares to go with the next group. He asks whether you want to join them. You can also wait for the scouts at the waystation.

## ev.road.fadlan_02.title

The Guide and the Snow Markers

## ev.road.fadlan_02.body

Beyond the last houses, the wind has covered the caravan road. A young driver walks ahead of the animals, searching for a marker his father described: three stones with a piece of red cord between them. He finds two stones, then begins scraping at the snow with his boot. “The old road turns here,” he says, though he cannot tell which way.

A local guide arrives with a bundle of firewood across his back. He brushes snow from the third stone and points toward a low ridge. “The cord blew away in autumn. I put the stone back after the first storm.” He knows a shelter beyond that ridge and a place where the animals can feed. He asks for a guiding fee for the next march. The caravan master says he has already paid for the animals' fodder and asks whether you would cover the guide's fee. The young driver watches the guide's hand on the stones, eager to learn the route.

## ev.road.fadlan_03.title

The Red Mist in the Old Journey

## ev.road.fadlan_03.body

The camp is nearly settled when the low sun reddens a bank of cloud. A herder is closing the sheep pen while his younger brother sits with an old copy of Ibn Fadlan’s account. The boy points at the sky. “Is this the red mist he saw?” His brother asks him to read the next line aloud. In the distant northern story, the light remained after dark and seemed to hold shapes of armed riders. “Will ours stay?” the boy asks. The herder ties the gate and comes over. “We can watch it while the sheep eat. Tell me what colour it is when I come back with the pail.” A companion offers to keep the passage open beside the fire and compare the boy’s account of this sunset with the older traveller’s words. Inside the tent, someone has saved a warm bowl for you. The boy shifts his seat so both the page and the clouds stay in view.

## ev.road.fadlan_04.title

The King's Story at the Fire

## ev.road.fadlan_04.body

At a roadside fire, a merchant unfolds a page he copied from an old travel account. It tells of a Bulghar king who went down to the Itil after traders reported a huge man by the flooded river. The account says he stood twelve cubits tall and had a head as large as a cooking pot. The king took him in and wrote to the people of Wīsū, three months away. The reply, according to the account, called the stranger one of Gog and Magog.

The merchant's niece has been turning a loaf over the coals. She asks who brought the reply from Wīsū, and her uncle checks the page. “It doesn't say. A traveller named Ibn Fadlan asked the king about the man and wrote down what he heard.” She breaks the loaf, gives him the larger piece, and asks him to read the part about the river again. Others edge closer to the fire. You can stay to ask about the old account, or take the road at first light.

## ev.road.fadlan_05.title

The Page About a Boat Funeral

## ev.road.fadlan_05.body

At a roadside camp, a young man has found a worn page inside his grandfather’s boatbuilding ledger. It carries Ibn Fadlan’s account of a Rus chieftain’s funeral long ago: a boat drawn ashore, new clothes sewn for the dead man, and an older woman the mourners called the Angel of Death. The grandfather used to build small river boats. “He folded this among the measurements for a keel,” the young man says. “I wish I had asked what he made of it.” His sister is coming to collect their grandfather’s cloak. She has sent word that the ledger ought to stay with the tools, but he wants her to see the page first. “Can you help me read the difficult names before she arrives?” he asks. A water carrier calls that she saw the sister waiting by the well; the young man also wants someone to walk with him there. He places the cloak beside the ledger so he will not leave either behind.

## ev.road.fadlan_06.title

The Sailor's Chart

## ev.road.fadlan_06.body

At a caravan halt, a sailor opens a chart on a crate and puts a pebble on each corner. His daughter has inked the coastal harbours from their last voyage. Along the edge, her father has copied older stories from Abū Hāmid’s account of the Caspian: a black mound said to rise from the water, birds nesting among snakes, and an island where travellers heard voices. Another traveller wrote of an island crowded with wild sheep. “Did you see any of that?” a driver asks the daughter. “I saw these harbours,” she says, touching the fresh ink. “The rest is what Father kept from the books.” Her father points to a blank inlet. “If you ever reach it, ask its name for us.” He offers the chart for a price. An inland merchant leaving at dawn calls that his pack animals have room beside them. The daughter waits for the ink to dry before she rolls the map, whether it will travel with you or stay in her hands.

## ev.road.fadlan_07.title

The Broker's Three Bundles

## ev.road.fadlan_07.body

A broker from Bulghar is repacking three bundles before the caravan leaves. His apprentice has tied beaver and ermine skins together. “The buyer asked for them separately,” the broker says, loosening the knot. From the third bundle he lifts a pale tusk so heavy that the apprentice catches its other end. “What animal carried a tooth like this?” the boy asks. The broker says people in the north dig such things out of the ground and send them south for carving. “One trader called them bones of ancient people,” he adds. “He had not seen the animal either.” The boy studies a small crack in the ivory and asks whether the buyer will mind. “Tell him about it before he pays,” the broker says. He asks you to hold the cord while they wrap the tusk and to help keep the two kinds of skins apart. A caller at the gate announces that the caravan is about to leave; the apprentice is still waiting to hear where the tusk will go.

## ev.road.fadlan_09.title

The Mound in the Travel Book

## ev.road.fadlan_09.body

At a steppe halt, a pilgrim has opened an old travel book on the bench. His daughter is copying a tiny drawing in its margin. The page tells of a pool near Khwarazm, a mound with a gold-tiled dome, and travellers who said a boat lowered into the water vanished. Another page has a tale of the giants of Iram and a tomb far away. “Which part did the person who wrote this see?” the girl asks. Her father goes back through the lines and admits that some came from other travellers. She rubs out the shape of a boat she had drawn too large. Beyond the halt, a low mound rises from the grass. “Come and draw those stones with me,” her father says. “I will carry paper and water for the walk.” Visiting the mound and returning to the road adds two days to your journey. The girl folds her unfinished sketch and asks whether you can help her name the parts she sees for herself.

## ev.road.fadlan_11.title

The Courier's Old Account

## ev.road.fadlan_11.body

At a roadside station, a courier is stitching the torn seam of his leather letter bag. A younger clerk holds an old account of Sallām the Interpreter open so the courier can listen while he works. Long before your journey, Sallām left Samarra in felt coats and fur boots to seek the barrier said to have been raised by Dhū al-Qarnayn. Letters introduced the travellers to rulers in Armenia, the land of the Alans, and among the Khazars. “Did all those letters fit in one bag?” the clerk asks. The courier pulls his new thread tight. “They needed only the letter for the next person. A wet seam would lose even that.” He asks the clerk to hold the bag to the light and check for gaps. The account also mentions a stretch of foul-smelling black earth. The courier offers to point out the old sequence of places before he closes the book; his own mail for the next station is drying on the table. The clerk keeps a finger on the page while you look at the repaired seam.

## ev.road.fadlan_13.title

The Two Rulers in an Old Book

## ev.road.fadlan_13.body

At a roadside camp, a copyist is reading Masʿudi’s older account to his apprentice while the ink dries on a fresh page. The account says that the Khazars had a ruling king who handled armies and public affairs, and a khaqan kept in the palace whose presence mattered to the kingship. When famine or defeat came, people sometimes demanded the khaqan’s death. The apprentice sets his pen down. “Did he decide any of this?” The copyist turns the book back a page. “The writer tells us what people did around him. Listen to the part where the king defended him.” The boy reads that line and looks at the clean margin. “Should I put his name here?” “We do not have it,” the copyist says. He asks you to hold the page flat while he makes a careful note of which part came from the old book. Outside, a carter calls that the animals are nearly ready; the apprentice waits for you to finish reading before he closes the cover.

## ev.road.fadlan_14.title

The Measure of a Short Night

## ev.road.fadlan_14.body

A travelling teacher’s geography book has come loose in his pack. He lays it open on a cloth and asks you to hold the pages while he threads them back together. The pupil sitting across from him reads Masʿudi’s account of summer nights around Bulghar, so short that a cooking pot might scarcely boil between dusk and dawn. “How long is our night?” she asks. The teacher hands her a marked stick. “Set this by the bowl at sunset. In the morning, we will see what you wrote.” She squints at the sky above the present camp. “I should write the place too.” “Yes, and the day,” he says, nudging his pen toward her. Watching and measuring here will add a day to your journey; travelling on with your party also uses another day along the road. The pupil presses the stick into level earth and waits for its shadow to lengthen.

A Turkic host offered you a bowl of koumiss fermented one day and one night — still mild, still lawful to drink without intoxication. He waited until you had tasted before he spoke of the road ahead. You accepted the bowl, drank once, and asked his blessing on the road, for to refuse the bowl is to refuse the tent.

## codex.cx-fadlan-salt.name

Salt and the Aral Shores

## codex.cx-fadlan-salt.body

A slow train of camels passed, each bearing slabs of salt crust from the shallow southern sea; the drivers said the water recedes in summer and leaves white fields hard enough to cut with an axe. They trade the salt north for fish and south for millet. Later you saw water shining on the horizon — reeds, birds, the ripple of wind — yet the nearer you rode, the more the ground crunched white underhoof. It was a salt flat, not a lake, and the caravan turned aside lest the hooves break through the shell into brine.

## codex.cx-fadlan-omen.name

The Steppe Eclipse

## codex.cx-fadlan-omen.body

Mid-march, the sun thinned to a crescent and the steppe darkened though it was not yet evening. Some riders shouted that heaven warned the caravan; others said the astronomers of Bukhara had predicted it. The horses shied and would not line until the light returned. You recorded the hour and what the riders said, and saw how the sky itself becomes an omen on open grass.

## codex.cx-fadlan-kurgan.name

An Opened Tumulus

## codex.cx-fadlan-kurgan.body

Beside the track rose a kurgan already breached — prior diggers had left bones and broken pottery scattered down the slope. The elders said the mound had held a horse lord of an earlier age. You surveyed the mound and sketched what the diggers left; nothing of value remained, only the wind in the hollow crown.

## codex.cx-steppe-road.name

The Steppe Road

## codex.cx-steppe-road.body

Before the Gate of the Turks, caravan masters spoke of an akilavuz — a guide bred to these roads, who knows where the snow lies knee-deep and which ribāt still keeps fodder; without him you may wander ten days on the steppe and meet no soul. The road itself is crossings: hide boats stretched on saddle frames where rivers run too swift for wading, a crossroads where Radhaniya and Rus routes meet, and low yurts where a sick child waits for water and herbs while the caravan master signals to move. You have written all of it down as part of the road.
