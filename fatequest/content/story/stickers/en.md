## sticker.st-aden-shrine.body

You keep a little mark of the mandir of Aden, taken at the gate of the spice road.
Aden is the haven where the ships of India come with their cargoes, and the people of
the province adore Mahommet; yet the temple door stood open to a stranger who came
with courtesy. You left as you entered, without asking price or promise, and word of
your conduct ran ahead of you along the quay. The mark is small, but it stays with
you, and it opens the way where no letter would.

## sticker.st-badashan-shrine.body

You keep the mark of the masjid of Badashan, the province whose kings trace their
blood to King Alexander and the daughter of King Darius, and call themselves
Zulcarniain in the Saracen tongue. The people worship Mahommet and speak a language
of their own. In the mountains the balas rubies are dug from great caves under the
earth, on the king's account alone; no one else dares dig in that mountain, on pain
of forfeiture of life as well as goods. You visited the mosque, and word of your
conduct ran ahead of you. The mark stays with you.

## sticker.st-balc-lapis.body

You keep a block of raw lapis lazuli from the mines of Badakhshan, twelve days east
of Balc, bought in the great caravanserai — a fortress-sized courtyard ringed with
stables for a thousand camels, its storerooms stacked with bales of silk and leather
sacks of the blue stone. The lapis is sold in rough blocks and traded for silver,
silk, and spices, and it is the city's chief treasure. The caravanserai never
sleeps; fires burn in the arcades all night, and the smell of lamb kebab and saffron
rice hangs in the air. You kept the block through those nights.

## sticker.st-balc-magi.body

You keep the fire of the Magi, which they say has never been extinguished since the
days of Zoroaster, a thousand years before the Prophet. They call the place the Tomb
of the Magi, and say the three wise men who visited the infant Christ are buried
here, in three stone sarcophagi that still bear traces of gold leaf. The Magi in
their white robes chant the ancient hymns at dawn and dusk, and pilgrims from
Persia, India, and distant Cathay leave offerings of incense and silver coins. The
dream you dreamed there, you keep to yourself.

## sticker.st-balc-ruins.body

You keep the memory of Bactra, the mother of cities, whose broken ramparts stand
about Balc. Alexander took Roxana of Sogdiana to wife within these walls, when the
city enclosed more ground than any in Asia and its temples held treasure from India,
Persia, and Greece. Now the domes are collapsed and the palaces empty, and the new
houses are smaller, built from the stones of the old. Yet the market is alive again,
and lapis, rubies, and Turkoman horses keep the city's heart beating. You carried
this away from the ruins, and it has not left you.

## sticker.st-balc-ruby.body

You keep a balas ruby from the mountains of Balc, a stone worth a ship in Venice. It
changed hands in the great caravanserai, where merchants of every nation keep their
chambers and the storerooms are stacked with bales of silk and leather sacks of lapis
from Badakhshan. The price it fetched would buy a vessel outright in Venice, and the
merchants who weighed it knew its worth to a grain. You turn it in your palm when the
road is long, and it still seems too small to be worth so much.

## sticker.st-baldacum-brocade.body

You keep a length of Baudas brocade worked with gold, woven in the city where they
make many silk stuffs and gold brocades — nasich, nac, and cramoisy — richly wrought
with figures of beasts and birds. It came out of the covered markets of Baudas,
where the silk-merchants keep one vaulted lane and the silversmiths another, and
each guild maintains its own mosque, bathhouse, and coffee-house. The money-changers
sit behind low tables covered with mountains of silver dirhams, and the merchants
drink sherbet cooled with snow from the Kurdish mountains. You keep the gold thread,
and the pattern of it.

## sticker.st-baldacum-fall.body

You keep the proof of the fall of Baudas, the story told in the bazaars. The Caliph,
trusting in his gold and his twenty thousand guards, refused to pay tribute to the
Great Khan. Hulagu marched with a host that blackened the plain from horizon to
horizon, broke the dikes of the Tigris, and drained the Caliph's moat. When the
walls fell, he shut the Caliph in his own treasury — a tower full of gold, silver,
jewels, and silk — and told him to eat his treasure. The Caliph starved among his
wealth, and the city burned for a month.

## sticker.st-baldacum-palace.body

You keep the emptiness of the Caliph's hall at Baudas, the great palace whose golden
dome could be seen three days' journey away across the flat plain of the Tigris. Its
halls were hung with silk embroidered with gold thread, its gardens watered by
channels drawn from the river, and its treasury held the tribute of a hundred
nations. The Caliph himself was never seen except by a handful of servants. When
Hulagu came to the walls, all that gold and all those swords could not save him. The
hall stands empty, and you carry the emptiness with you.

## sticker.st-cail-shrine.body

You carry away a small sign of the mandir of Cail, and your name carries a little
more weight in the city for it. Cail is the great and noble city where the ships
from Hormos, from Kis, from Aden, and from all Arabia touch, laden with horses and
with other things for sale, and the King favours merchants and foreigners. A
stranger is watched here, and a stranger who enters the temple with courtesy is
spoken of. The small sign of the place stays with you, a good word laid up against
the day of need.

## sticker.st-calatu-shrine.body

You keep the mark of the mandir of Calatu, the strong city on the gulf where the
Melic of Hormos takes refuge in time of war. The people are Saracens subject to
Hormos, and they grow no corn here but get it from abroad. The haven is large and
good, frequented by ships with goods from India, and from this city the spices are
distributed among the cities of the interior. You visited the temple, and word of
your conduct ran ahead of you. The little mark stays with you.

## sticker.st-camadi-shrine.body

You carry away a small sign of the masjid of Camadi, and your name carries a little
more weight in the plain for it. Camadi was formerly a great and noble place, but
now it is of little consequence, for the Tartars in their incursions have several
times ravaged it. The plain is a very hot region, and the fruits of the country are
dates, pistachioes, and apples of Paradise. The sign of the place stays with you,
with the memory of how much a city may lose and still stand.

## sticker.st-cambaluc-court.body

You keep the great hall of the Kaan at Cambaluc, the largest roofed building in the
world, where six thousand men can sit at table when the Kaan holds his feasts. The
roof is painted vermilion, blue, green, and gold; dragons coil around the beams and
phoenixes spread their wings across the ceiling. The walls are hung with silk
embroidered with hunts on horseback and battles between elephants and tigers. The
Kaan sits on a raised dais at the northern end, on a throne of carved jade, so that
his gaze falls on every guest.

## sticker.st-cambaluc-hill.body

You keep the art of the green hill of Cambaluc, raised in the Kaan's great park of
green jade, jasper, and lapis lazuli, brought block by block from the quarries of
Badakhshan and Cotan. The trees upon it were dug up by the roots in distant forests,
wrapped in silk and wet earth, and carried here on the backs of elephants; they grow
as green as in their native soil. A thousand gardeners water them daily by hand, and
the Kaan walks among them alone in the evenings, when no one may disturb him. You
keep the hill.

## sticker.st-cambaluc-suburbs.body

You keep the counting of the twelve suburbs of Cambaluc, each larger than most
cities of Christendom or Islam. The merchants of every province of the Kaan's empire
— Cathay, Manzi, Tangut, Tibet, the Golden Horde, Persia, and India — keep their
warehouses and counting-houses there; a thousand cart-loads of silk enter the city
every day, and forty cart-loads of pepper and spice are consumed daily. No poor
person goes hungry, for the Kaan maintains public granaries that distribute rice and
millet to anyone who asks. You counted the cart-loads with the tally-men, and you
keep the sum of it.

## sticker.st-campichu-shrine.body

You keep the mark of the sì of Campichu, the capital of Tangut, where the Idolaters
keep an enormous number of idols in their minsters and abbeys. Some of the idols are
a good ten paces in stature, of wood, clay, or stone, all highly polished and
covered with gold; the great ones lie at length, with figures round about as if
adoring before them. Religious recluses among the people lead a more virtuous life
than the rest. You visited the monastery, and word of your conduct ran ahead of you.
The little mark stays with you.

## sticker.st-camul-shrine.body

You keep the mark of the masjid of Camul, the first oasis east of the Great Desert,
where the traveler who has survived the sand begins to breathe again. The people are
Idolaters; they once practiced a strange custom of offering their wives to passing
strangers as a mark of hospitality, until the Great Khan forbade it, though the old
people still speak of it. You visited the mosque, and word of your conduct ran ahead
of you. The melons of this district are the sweetest on the whole road, and the
little mark stays with you.

## sticker.st-cascar-carpet.body

You keep a carpet of Cascar, woven so fine that a man can pass it through a
finger-ring. The women worked for months on it, knowing every dye by its smell:
madder-root for red, indigo for blue, pomegranate rind for yellow, walnut hull for
brown, and a secret mineral for a green that fades only after a hundred years. The
patterns are said to contain prayers, protecting the owner from the evil eye, from
snake-bite, and from the spirits of the desert. A merchant who buys a Cascar carpet
never travels without it, and neither do you.

## sticker.st-cascar-garden.body

You keep the garden of the Tarim, the richest oasis on the Silk Road, where the
melt-water from the Pamir and the Karakorum feeds a green paradise in the desert.
Orchards of apricot, pomegranate, mulberry, walnut, and almond stretch for miles
along the irrigation canals, and the vines grow so heavy with grapes that the
trellises must be replaced every year. The people worship Mahommet and keep many old
customs from before the time of the Prophet. You tasted the melons of Cascar — the
sweetest and largest in the world — and have spoken of nothing else since.

## sticker.st-cascar-sleepers.body

You keep the cave of the Seven Sleepers, cut in a limestone cliff outside Cascar,
where seven holy men are said to sleep. The Muslims say they have slept since before
the time of the Prophet, waiting for the Day of Judgment; the Christians say they
are their own Seven Sleepers of Ephesus, miraculously transported here. A mosque and
a chapel stand side by side at the cave entrance, and the monks and the mullahs take
turns sweeping the cave, the offerings of candles, incense, and silver coins divided
equally. You carried away both names of the sleepers.

## sticker.st-chamba-shrine.body

You keep the mark of the masjid of Chamba, the rich country reached by sailing
west-south-west from Zayton for fifteen hundred miles. The people are Idolaters and
pay the Great Kaan a yearly tribute of elephants and nothing but elephants, ever
since his Baron Sagatu came with a great force of horse and foot against their aged
king, who saw the havoc and sent messengers to make his peace. You visited the
mosque, and word of your conduct ran ahead of you. The little mark stays with you, a
quiet name in a land that ransomed itself with elephants.

## sticker.st-chandu-mares.body

You keep the sacred white mares of Chandu, the herd of a thousand that are not
ridden or worked, but milked twice a day in golden vessels for the Kaan's table; no
one of lesser rank may taste it. The monks of the Palace of the Spring tend them in
the meadow at the park's northern end. Once a year, at the great Mongol festival,
the Kaan pours the milk on the ground as an offering to the spirits of the Eternal
Blue Sky and the Golden Earth. No foreigner has ever witnessed this ceremony.

## sticker.st-chandu-palace.body

You keep the gilded cane palace of Chandu, the summer palace of the Great Kaan,
built entirely of cane — the columns, the walls, everything. The cane is harvested
from a forest in the south of Manzi, split, lacquered, gilded with gold leaf, and
lashed together with silken cords, so it can be taken down and moved when the Kaan
travels. Dragons in green, blue, vermilion, and gold coil up every pillar, and the
marble floors are so polished they reflect the gilded ceiling. No other building is
so light and yet so splendid. You keep it as it stood.

## sticker.st-chandu-park.body

You keep the Kaan's game park at Chandu, enclosed by a wall that runs for sixteen
miles, with a forest so dense that a man on horseback cannot cross it in a single
day. It is stocked with deer, boar, wild ass, bear, and leopard, and a lake holds
every species of waterfowl known to man. The Kaan rides here with his falconers,
huntsmen, and cheetah-keepers, and you watched his gerfalcons fed in their mews. In
one corner the thousand white mares are kept, so sacred that no one of lower rank
may ride them, touch them, or walk in their pasture.

## sticker.st-chinangli-shrine.body

You carry away a small sign of the sì of Chinangli, and your name carries a little
more weight there now. The city belongs to the Great Kaan and stands on a great and
wide river, on which a large traffic in silk goods and spices passes up and down;
the people are Idolaters and have paper-money. Five days south, the towns burn their
dead and live by trade and handicrafts, in great abundance. The sign of the place
stays with you, and with it the memory of a great river busy with silk and spices.

## sticker.st-cotan-buddha.body

You keep the dust of the buried city on the old river terrace beyond Cotan, where
the water no longer flows. The sand has buried an older city — a thousand
monasteries, a hundred stupas, and painted caves beyond counting. The wind uncovers
fragments each day: a painted face of a bodhisattva with kohl-rimmed eyes, a hand
raised in blessing, lotus petals in gold and vermilion. The old people drink this
dust as medicine, and the desert reclaims whatever the expeditions of Cambaluc
uncover. You keep the dust, and the names of the buried monasteries.

## sticker.st-cotan-jade.body

You keep a piece of Cotan jade — white like congealed mutton-fat, green like the
first leaves of spring. It comes from the two rivers that carry the stones down
from the mountains. In autumn, when the rivers run low, the men wade into the cold
water and feel for the stones with their bare feet, diving to bring them up. The
largest stones go to the Kaan's agents, to be carved in Cambaluc into cups,
bracelets, and belt-buckles, and a fine piece is worth its weight in gold. You keep
a piece of it.

## sticker.st-cotan-rivers.body

You keep the reading of the rivers above Cotan, where the jade comes down from the
mountains — white jade like congealed mutton-fat, and green jade like the first
leaves of spring. In autumn, when the rivers run low, the men of Cotan wade into
the cold water and feel for the stones with their bare feet; when they find one,
they dive and bring it up. The divers read the water — its depth, its season, its
gravel — as other men read a page. You learned that reading from them, and you keep
it.

## sticker.st-cotan-silk.body

You keep a bolt of Cotan silk, spun finer than any in Cathay — a single thread,
finer than a human hair, can run for a thousand paces without breaking. The country
round is all mulberry trees, and the silk is dyed with saffron for yellow, indigo
for blue, madder for red, and cochineal for crimson. At the looms, two women pass
the shuttle back and forth while singing a song that keeps the rhythm. A bolt large
enough for a robe sells for its weight in silver in Cambaluc's bazaars. You keep
the bolt, and the song with it.

## sticker.st-esher-shrine.body

You carry away a small sign of the mandir of Esher, and your name carries a little
more weight there now. Esher lies four hundred miles from the Port of Aden, under a
king who is subject to the Soldan; the people are Saracens, and the haven is very
good, with ships from India coming and chargers going out. White incense grows in
this country, and no one dares sell it but the Prince, who takes it from the people
at ten livres the hundredweight and sells it at sixty. The small sign of the place
stays with you.

## sticker.st-kenjanfu-shrine.body

You keep the mark of the sì of Kenjanfu, the capital of a kingdom that in old times
was noble, rich, and powerful, and is now held by Mangalai, son of the Great Kaan,
who hath crowned him king thereof. The country round is planted with mulberries, on
the leaves of which the silkworms feed, and there is plenty of game of all sorts,
both of beasts and birds. You visited the monastery, and word of your conduct ran
ahead of you through the city of great trade and industry. The little mark stays
with you.

## sticker.st-kerman-shrine.body

You keep the mark of the masjid of Kerman, the kingdom of Persia where the
turquoises are found in great abundance in the mountains, and where the best falcons
in the world are found. The people are very skilful in making harness of war —
saddles, bridles, spurs, swords, bows, and quivers — and the ladies of the country
work silk embroidery with figures of beasts and birds, trees and flowers, so deftly
that their hangings are marvels to see. You visited the mosque, and word of your
conduct ran ahead of you. The little mark stays with you.

## sticker.st-keshimur-shrine.body

You keep the mark of the masjid of Keshimur, the province men say is the very
source from which Idolatry spread abroad. The people are Idolaters, with a language
of their own, and they have an astonishing acquaintance with enchantment: they make
their idols to speak, and by their sorceries bring changes of weather and darkness.
The clime is finely tempered, neither very hot nor very cold, and the strong passes
let the country keep its independence. You visited the mosque, and word of your
conduct ran ahead of you. The mark stays with you.

## sticker.st-kinsay-canals.body

You keep the twelve thousand bridges of Kinsay, the city built on water, where the
streets are canals and the boats are the carriages. The bridges are of stone, and so
tall that a ship with its mast raised can pass beneath without lowering the yard. A
man could spend his whole life in Kinsay and never walk more than a hundred paces on
dry land. From the drum-tower, a hundred canals radiate, their embankments straight
as a sword-cut. The city has no walls — the water is its wall, guarded by the Kaan's
fleet of patrol boats.

## sticker.st-kinsay-lake.body

You keep the West Lake of Kinsay as it stood by moonlight, when the lanterns hung
from the awning poles. The pleasure boats float day and night, painted in gold,
blue, and vermilion — each boat a floating pavilion, with musicians playing the pipa
and the guqin, and dancers in costumes embroidered with phoenixes. The lake is so
large that a circuit takes a whole day, and its islands hold pleasure-palaces. A man
who has not seen the West Lake by moonlight has not seen the most beautiful thing in
the Kaan's empire.

## sticker.st-kinsay-markets.body

You keep the account of the ten great markets of Kinsay, each one half a mile on a
side, surrounded by double-storied warehouses. Every fish of sea and lake, every
fruit of the earth, every fabric, spice, drug, and perfume is sold in one of the ten
squares, and forty cart-loads of pepper pass the customs every day. The main street
is so finely paved that no water stands on it. On either side are bathhouses and
restaurants, where a man bathes in perfumed water and dines on ten courses for a few
copper coins.

## sticker.st-lop-jade.body

You keep the jade of Lop, bought from the dealer who spreads his stones on black
velvet. In a low voice he told you where the jade comes from: the riverbeds above
Cotan, where the streams run down from the snow mountains and lay their gravel bare
when the autumn waters fall, so that one who knows the season buys at the source
for a tenth of the price. A piece of mutton-fat white jade the size of a man's fist
costs more than a camel, and light passes through it like water. You keep his word
with the stone.

## sticker.st-melibar-shrine.body

You keep the mark of the mandir of Melibar, the great kingdom towards the west whose
people are Idolaters, with a language and a king of their own, and pay tribute to
nobody. From this kingdom and from Gozurat more than a hundred corsair vessels go
forth every year, joining in fleets of twenty or thirty that cover some hundred
miles of sea, signalling one to another by fire or smoke, so that no merchant ship
can escape them. You visited the temple, and word of your conduct ran ahead of you.
The little mark stays with you.

## sticker.st-ormus-fort.body

You keep the fortress of Hormos, guarding the strait where the Persian Gulf opens
into the Sea of India. Its walls of sun-baked brick and coral stone are thick enough
to turn any mangonel, and its cisterns hold enough water to withstand a year's
siege. Every ship that enters the harbor pays its toll in pepper and silk; the
customs officer sits in a shaded alcove by the quay, weighing every bale on a brass
scale. The city lives on the sea — every man is a sailor, a pearl-diver, or a
merchant. You stood there, and you keep it.

## sticker.st-ormus-spice.body

You keep the first spice from the Indies bought at Hormos, where pepper, cinnamon,
ginger, nutmeg, cloves, and cardamom arrive on Arab dhows and Chinese junks. The
warehouses are stacked with sacks marked in seven scripts, and the heat is so great
a candle left inside will melt; men work at dawn and sleep through the midday. A
cargo of pepper bought here for ten thousand bezants sells for sixty thousand in
Venice, and the merchant princes' houses have wind-towers that funnel the sea
breeze into cool underground chambers. You keep the spice, and the smell of the
monsoon.

## sticker.st-ormus-wind.body

You keep the warning of the Wind of Death at Hormos, so hot that men caught in the
open drop dead where they stand. The houses are built with tall towers that catch
the slightest breeze and funnel it down into underground chambers; on the worst days
the whole population sits in the irrigation channels up to their chins — men, women,
animals together — and the dead are buried at night. A merchant who plans his
arrival for the summer finds the city half-deserted, the warehouses shuttered, and
the harbor empty of ships. You keep the warning.

## sticker.st-sachiu.body

You carry away a small sign of Sachiu, the city on the edge of the Great Desert,
reached after thirty days of crossing, in the province of Tangut. The people are
Idolaters, with a peculiar language; they are no traders, but live by the fruits of
the earth, and their many abbeys and minsters are full of idols of divers fashions,
to which they pay great honour. Here the caravans restock after the terrible
crossing, and the irrigated fields grow excellent melons and wheat. You set what you
saw into your travel book, and the sign stays with you.

## sticker.st-saianfu-shrine.body

You carry away a small sign of the sì of Saianfu, and your name carries a little
more weight there now. Saianfu is a very great and noble city, ruling over twelve
other large and rich cities, and is itself a seat of great trade and manufacture,
with much silk, from which they weave fine stuffs. It held out against the Great
Kaan for three years after the rest of Manzi had surrendered, for the great and deep
waters round about it let his troops approach from the north side only. The sign of
the place stays with you.

## sticker.st-samarcanda-dome.body

You keep a place beneath the turquoise dome of the Gur-i-Mir, rising above the old
city like a second sky. Samarcand is called the garden of the world: its streets are
lined with canals shaded by plane trees, every courtyard has a fountain, every
mosque a turquoise dome, and the Registan's three great madrasas are the heart of
learning in Central Asia, where scholars come from every country of Islam to study
law, theology, astronomy, and medicine. Between Baghdad and Cambaluc there is no
city whose name carries more weight with the caravans.

## sticker.st-samarcanda-gardens.body

You keep the walk of the canals of Samarcand, the city they call the garden of the
world. Its streets are lined with canals shaded by plane trees, and every courtyard
has a fountain, every mosque a turquoise dome. The Registan square, with its three
great madrasas, is the heart of learning in Central Asia; scholars come from every
country of Islam to study law, theology, astronomy, and medicine under the
blue-tiled arches. Between Baghdad and Cambaluc there is no city richer, no city
more beautiful. You walked its canals, and you keep the green of them.

## sticker.st-samarcanda-paper.body

You keep a sheaf of Samarcand paper, the best in the world, beaten and polished
smooth with stone until it shines like watered silk. The secret was learned from
Chinese prisoners taken in battle, when the armies of Islam first crossed the Oxus,
but they have surpassed their teachers — the paper is whiter, smoother, and more
durable than any made in Cathay. A single sheet can cost a day's wages for a skilled
worker, but the calligraphers say it is worth it: ink never bleeds on Samarcand
paper, and a book written on it will last three hundred years.

## sticker.st-samarcanda-stone.body

You keep the mark of the weeping stone of Samarcand, the pillar of black basalt in
the courtyard of the great mosque, brought here, they say, by the Prophet himself.
It is polished by centuries of hands touching it for blessing. They say the stone
weeps before disaster — drops of water appear on its surface, and within a year war,
plague, or earthquake strikes the city. The last time it wept was the year before
the Mongol rider first appeared on the northern horizon. You pressed your forehead
to it, and the mark stays with you.

## sticker.st-siju-shrine.body

You carry away a small sign of the sì of Siju, and your name carries a little more
weight there now. Siju is a great, rich, and noble city, flourishing with trade and
manufactures; the people are Idolaters, burn their dead, use paper-money, and are
subjects of the Great Kaan, and their extensive and fertile plains produce abundance
of wheat and other grain. Three days south the great river Caramoran flows down from
Prester John's country. The sign of the place stays with you.

## sticker.st-sinju-shrine.body

You carry away a small sign of the sì of Sinju, and your name carries a little more
weight there now. Sinju is a city of no great size, but it possesses a very great
amount of shipping and trade, for it stands on the greatest river in the world — the
Kian — which is in some places ten miles wide, in others eight, in others six, and
more than a hundred days' journey in length. On its waters merchandize is
perpetually coming and going, enriching the city. The sign of the place stays with
you.

## sticker.st-suju-shrine.body

You carry away a small sign of the sì of Suju, and your name carries a little more
weight there now. Suju is a very great and noble city of some sixty miles in
circuit, whose people are accomplished traders and most skilful craftsmen, making
gold brocade and other stuffs from their great quantity of silk; there are also many
philosophers and leeches. The city has six thousand bridges, all of stone, and so
lofty that a galley, or even two galleys at once, could pass underneath one of them.
The sign of the place stays with you.

## sticker.st-taican-shrine.body

You keep the mark of the masjid of Taican, the fortified place with a great corn
market, where the mountains are all composed of salt. It is the best in the world,
so hard that it can only be broken with iron picks, and so abundant that it would
supply the whole world to the end of time; people from all the countries round, to
some thirty days' journey, come to fetch it. Other mountains grow almonds and
pistachioes, exceedingly cheap. You visited the mosque, and word of your conduct ran
ahead of you. The little mark stays with you.

## sticker.st-tanpiju-shrine.body

You carry away a small sign of the sì of Tanpiju, and your name carries a little
more weight there now. Tanpiju is a great, rich, and fine city under Kinsay, a
day's journey to the south-east through a plenteous region of dwellings and charming
gardens. The people are subject to the Kaan, have paper-money, and are Idolaters who
burn their dead; they live by trade and manufactures and handicrafts, and have all

## sticker.st-tauris-gardens.body

You keep the gardens of Tauris, stretching for miles along the qanats that bring
water from the mountains. The great lords of the Ilkhanate — Persian viziers, Mongol
noyans, Armenian merchants, and Turkish amirs — keep their summer pavilions here:
open halls with tiled fountains, shaded by plane trees and cypress, surrounded by
orchards of apricot, pomegranate, almond, and pistachio. The air smells of jasmine,
rose, and watered earth, and the fruit is the finest in Persia. The gardeners say: a
man who drinks the water of Tauris and eats its apricots will never want to leave.

## sticker.st-tauris-pearl.body

You keep a Tabriz pearl from the covered market of Tauris, where the Moslem traders
sell silk from China and Cathay, pearls from the Gulf, and cloth of gold from
Baghdad. It came to you at the toll-master's price, in a city where nearly every
foreign merchant-house keeps an agent — the Genoese in their fortress-like fondaco
near the Great Bazaar, the Armenians in their caravanserai by the east gate. Every
bale that passes through the city pays a tax to the Ilkhan, and you paid yours. The
pearl is small, the price was fair, and it stays with you.

## sticker.st-tauris-road-sense.body

You keep the road warning of Tauris, taken from the foreign merchants who do not
merely name places. One points south toward Ctesiphon and marks the road in
charcoal; another warns that the road to Baudas follows a different rhythm of tolls
and water; and a third keeps a water ledger that gives you one more day's measure
between wells. Their answers were useful only because you pressed them for the
detail that belongs to your own journey — the tolls, the wells, the levy. You carry
the warning with you, and the next departure is no longer blind.

## sticker.st-tauris-view.body

You keep the view from the Ilkhan's hill at Tauris, where the whole plain lies open
— a green island in the brown Persian plateau, dotted with the smoke of a thousand
cooking-fires. The city has outgrown every wall ever built around it, and its
suburbs stretch for miles across the plain, a patchwork of orchards, workshops,
gardens, and caravanserais, where Persian, Turkish, Armenian, Georgian, and Frankish
merchants trade the goods of two continents. Below the palace, gardens of cypress
and rose are watered by qanats that bring snow-melt from the mountains. You keep
that view.

## sticker.st-tenduc-shrine.body

You carry away a small sign of the ovoo of Tenduc, the sacred cairn, and your name
carries a little more weight there now. Tenduc is the province whose king, George by
name, is of the lineage of Prester John and holds his land under the Great Kaan; the
rule is in the hands of the Christians, though there are plenty of Idolaters and
worshippers of Mahommet. Here is found the stone from which Azure is made, and fine
camlets of different colours are woven from camel's hair. The sign of the sacred
cairn stays with you.

## sticker.st-zayton-haven.body

You keep the haven of Zayton, where the quay is never quiet. Pepper comes in sacks
the height of a man; pearls weighed in brass scales, lignaloes and sandalwood
stacked like cordwood. The merchant pays dearly here: the Kaan takes a tamghā on
everything, ten in the hundred, plus the ship's charge — thirty in the hundred on
small wares, forty on bulky, four and forty on pepper. Between the freight and the
Kaan's due a man pays out a good half of his cargo's worth, yet on the other half
the profit is so great that he is glad to come back with more.

## sticker.st-zayton-mazu.body

You keep the smoke of the sea-temple of Zayton, above the anchorage, where no
shipmaster of this coast puts out without going up to it first. They burn paper
there, and set out a dish of rice and a cup of wine, and shake a bamboo cylinder of
numbered slips until one falls; the slip is carried to an old woman by the door, who
reads what is written against that number. The sailors do not call this
fortune-telling. They call it asking. You stood at the back and watched, and you
keep the asking with you.

## sticker.st-zayton-silk.body

You keep a bolt of silk bought from a Manzi weaver in the foreign quarter of Zayton,
where the merchants of India, of Persia, and of the Arab lands have their own houses
of prayer, long enough that their grandsons are buried in this soil. Persian and
Arabic and the local tongue are bargained in under one roof, and a man who has none
will find his purse lighter than his understanding. Nobody in the quarter finds any
of this remarkable, which is itself the most remarkable thing about it. You keep the
silk, and the lesson.
