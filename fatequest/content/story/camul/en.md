## city.camul.name

Camul

## ev.camul.entry.body

At the Hami oasis, a cart driver is counting the water skins beside a melon stall. His daughter has marked each one with a strip of cloth so the family will not confuse drinking water with water for washing the animals. She finds two with the same colour and asks him which he filled at the well. ‘The heavy one,’ he says, then lifts both and admits he cannot tell.

The melon seller calls them over. She calls the morning water carrier over. He recognises the patched strap on the skin he filled at the sweet well, and the seller gives the girl a fresh strip of cloth. You ask where a traveller can sleep. The seller points to a lodging yard; the driver says its owner keeps the animals under shade, though the courtyard fills quickly when caravans arrive.

The girl ties the new strip and asks whether you have room for a melon. Her father laughs: ‘First make sure your own water is marked.’ You can mark Hami and go on, spend a day among the market stalls, or visit the local place of worship and meet the people who care for it.

## ev.camul.a.title

The Melon Seller’s Water Turn

## ev.camul.a.body

At the melon market, the seller Nuri is tying a reed tag to a basket. Her brother has been tending the water gate and says the next family has asked to open it early. Their melon vines are wilting. Nuri checks the wooden turn board: her own garden still has an hour of water left. ‘If they take it now, the end of our row stays dry,’ she says.

A child from the neighbouring garden arrives carrying a cut melon. She asks whether Nuri will trade the hour for this one. Nuri smiles but tells her the board belongs to everyone, so the adults must agree. You ask how they keep track. Her brother shows you the marks for each garden and the small channels leading from the well.

The child sits beside the baskets to wait for her mother. Nuri cuts a slice and holds it up for you to see while they talk. You can spend the day walking the beds and learn their watering routine, buy a small piece for 0.6 silver and eat it, or ask the drivers which roads lead to Lop and Suzhou.

## ev.camul.b.title

The Courtyard Keeper’s Guest

## ev.camul.b.body

An older keeper is sweeping sand from the courtyard of a local shrine. His nephew has laid out mats for travellers, but one has a torn edge and catches on everyone’s shoes. The keeper asks him to take it back inside. ‘The visitors can use the good ones while I mend this,’ he says.

A woman arrives with her father, who needs shade after the road. She asks whether they may sit for a moment. The keeper pours tea and tells his nephew to bring the low stool. The boy asks about an old local tale of how their families once welcomed passing strangers. ‘I heard it from my uncle,’ the keeper answers. ‘First find this gentleman somewhere comfortable to sit. We can tell the story while he rests.’

You help move the stool and ask whether the old man would like more water. He says he would rather hear where the next well lies. You can stay a day to hear the keeper’s account, quietly observe the courtyard gathering, or ask him about the eastern road toward Etzina.

## ev.ev_camul_a_followup.title

Nuri’s Packed Melons

## ev.ev_camul_a_followup.body

At Nuri’s stall, she and her brother are tying a full merchant load of melons for the next caravan. The neighbours have finished their water turn, and the child who carried the cut melon is drawing small marks on each basket so her mother can identify her family’s share. Nuri checks each knot before the driver loads it.

‘You used to mix these baskets up,’ Nuri tells the child. ‘I can read the marks now,’ the girl replies. You ask where the caravan will go. The driver names Lop and Suzhou and shows a worn list of wells; another party is waiting for news from Etzina. Nuri says the full load is available for 108 silver. The driver warns that this is trade cargo, not a snack for the road.

The child hands her mother’s basket over first. You can buy Nuri’s merchant lot, ask her and the driver to go through the wells with you, or stay a day for the eastbound party to arrive and ask them about Etzina.

## ev.ev_camul_a_followup.choice_2_result

## ev.ev_camul_a_followup.choice_3

## ev.camul.waterboard.title

The Missing Water Board

## ev.camul.waterboard.body

At a narrow channel beyond the market, two children are looking for the board that shows whose garden receives water next. Their grandfather set it beside the sluice, but the wind has carried it into the reeds. One child wants to guess from yesterday’s order; the other says their neighbours swapped turns this morning.

You ask the grandfather whether the board can be replaced. ‘We can make another,’ he says, ‘but the old marks will tell us what everyone agreed.’ A woman from the next garden arrives with a jar of drinking water and asks if they have found it. The younger child spots a corner of wood by the bank. All three lean down, and the grandfather warns them to hold the gate before anyone pulls the board free.

You can spend a day helping them recover and repaint the turn marks with the neighbours, or listen as the grandfather explains each mark and write down how the oasis shares the channel.

## ev.camul.waterboard.choice.restore

Spend a day restoring the water board

## ev.camul.waterboard.result.restore

You help repaint the marks and wait while each neighbour checks their agreed turn. At dusk the children hang the board under shelter. Their grandfather thanks you, and the families along the channel know your name.

## ev.camul.waterboard.choice.learn

Ask how the turn marks work

## ev.camul.waterboard.result.learn

The grandfather explains the marks while the children put the board back. You write down the watering order and the way they record changes, then leave them to open the gate for the next garden.
