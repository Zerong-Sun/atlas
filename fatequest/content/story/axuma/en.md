## city.axuma.name

Axuma

## ev.axuma.entry.body

At the edge of Axuma, a young woman is shifting water jars away from a sun-warmed stone monument. Her father, a church reader, watches her work with a small copper coin in his palm. “Will you tell our visitor why you keep that?” she asks him. He shows you the worn letters and says that kings once struck coins here; some travelled across the sea, while others remained in local hands. “Can this one still buy bread?” you ask. “My daughter says it can buy a story,” he replies, “and then she makes me carry her jars.” She laughs and explains that travellers have been taking the wrong track beyond the stones and arriving after the water seller has closed his shed. She points to the main road with her chin. “If you go now, take the broad turn by the fig tree. If you stay, help me put these jars in the shade before the afternoon heat gets to them.” The church reader slips the coin away and picks up the last jar himself.

## ev.axuma.entry.title

The Water Jars at Axuma

## ev.ev_axuma_entry.choice_1_result

The church reader walks with you as far as the fig tree and points to the road onward. His daughter calls after him to remember the empty jar he promised to return. He goes back for it, waving you on. You know where the station lies when you need water again.

## ev.ev_axuma_entry.choice_2_result

You stay for a day and help move the jars under the roof. The daughter brings bread and a bowl of lentils when the work is done. “You saved me two trips in the sun,” she says. After a full night's rest you leave with your strength restored and with the station's turn fixed in mind.

## ev.axuma.site_gap.title

The Water Seller’s Cracked Jar

## ev.axuma.site_gap.body

A water seller near the old stones holds a large jar against the light. A hairline crack runs from its rim, and his niece is already kneading clay to seal it. “If I fill it now, it will leak all the way to the road,” he tells you. She sets a smaller jar beside him. “Use this one for the travellers today. I can mend the large one after we close.” He worries that the smaller jar will run dry before the next caravan. You ask where he draws water. “From the spring beyond the southern rise,” she says, “but the road to Dongola continues farther west. People confuse the two forks.” She takes a piece of charcoal and marks both paths on the flat side of a broken tile. The uncle tries to take it from her with wet hands; she makes him dry them first. Then she asks if you can hold the jar steady while she binds the cracked rim. Her uncle promises to carry the next full jar himself once the patch has set. The girl asks her uncle to mark the western turn more clearly. He scratches a small well beside it on the tile, a landmark the travellers can ask about on the road.

## ev.axuma.site_gap.choice.route

Ask about the Dongola road

## ev.ev_axuma_site_gap.choice_1_result

The niece takes you to the fork and points out the stones that mark the Dongola road. “The spring path ends among the trees; this one keeps going,” she says. You trace the turn for yourself before returning her tile.

## ev.axuma.site_gap.choice.interact

Help bind the cracked jar

## ev.ev_axuma_site_gap.choice_2_result

You hold the jar while she presses clay into the crack and winds a strip of cloth around the rim. Her uncle waits until the patch has dried before pouring in a little water. It holds. He explains how he checks a jar before trusting it with a day's supply.

## ev.axuma.parchment.title

Two Pages at the Church Door

## ev.axuma.parchment.body

At the church door, a reader has spread two loose manuscript leaves on a clean cloth. The wind keeps turning one over. His nephew pins the corners with pebbles and says, “You told me to carry them separately. I did. Now you say they belong together.” The reader shows him the end of a line on one leaf and its continuation on the other. “I should have shown you this before you went across town,” he admits. They must return the leaves to an elderly copyist whose eyes are tired; he wants the nephew to know their order before they leave. The boy asks you to hold the cloth while he reads the join aloud. He stops at an unfamiliar word, and the reader pronounces it slowly. The boy tries again and gets through the sentence. “She'll ask you, not me, which page comes first,” the reader tells him. The nephew looks at you, waiting to hear whether his answer sounds right. The nephew points to the line where the pages meet. The reader asks him to say it once more without help; this time he knows why the second page begins there.

## ev.axuma.parchment.choice.help

Help carry the leaves to the copyist

## ev.axuma.parchment.result.help

You carry the cloth while the nephew keeps the leaves from lifting in the wind. At the copyist's house he tells her which page comes first and shows her the joining line. She thanks you both, then asks him to read it once more at a slower pace. Word of the help reaches the reader at the church.

## ev.axuma.parchment.choice.stay

Stay and practise reading with them

## ev.axuma.parchment.result.stay

You spend the day at the copyist's house. She gives the boy a clean scrap to practise on and lets you try the same line; whenever either of you rushes, she taps the table. At supper she shares lentils and warm bread. You leave rested, with the two leaves safely back in order.
