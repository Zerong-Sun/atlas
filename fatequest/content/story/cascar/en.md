## city.cascar.name

Cascar

## ev.cascar.a.body

In an orchard outside the market, grower Rima is checking a narrow channel with her daughter. Their neighbour’s branch takes water first today, but a loose stone has let some spill into the path. ‘Should I block it?’ the girl asks. Rima shakes her head. ‘Call him over. It is his turn, and he should see what we mend.’ The neighbour comes with a small spade and thanks them for waiting.

You ask whether the fruit has suffered. Rima points to one vine whose leaves are still cool, then to a younger row that needs the afternoon flow. The neighbour offers to lift the stone while you hold the channel gate. Her daughter has picked a melon for sale at 0.5 silver and packed a parcel of dried grapes for 1 silver. She asks which will travel better, and Rima lets her answer after she has carried both across the yard.

The water begins to run where it should. Rima invites you to stay a day and see the orchard after its turn; her daughter keeps the two parcels in the shade until you decide.

## ev.cascar.b.body

At a weaving bench, Saliha is finishing a carpet while her aunt stirs a dye vat outside. Her younger brother has copied the border pattern onto scrap cloth, but he has left out the small turn at one corner. ‘It will still lie flat,’ he says. Saliha shows him the finished edge. ‘Yes, but your grandmother will know which hand made it.’ He tries again, this time watching her fingers.

You ask whether the pattern belongs to their family. The aunt says they learned it from her mother and have changed it for different buyers. She sets out a packet of dye samples for 1.5 silver; Saliha has one finished trading carpet priced at 116 silver. Her brother asks whether buyers care about the small turn. One waiting customer says he did not notice until Saliha pointed it out, but now he wants to see the whole border.

Saliha invites you to sit for a day if you wish to learn the work. The aunt checks the vat, and the customer bends down to follow the pattern around the corner.

## ev.cascar.c.body

At a cave outside Kashgar, keeper Mariam is sweeping the space where visitors sit. An older Muslim neighbour brings a lamp; a Christian traveller arrives with a small candle. Both ask where they may set them. Mariam points to separate shelves, then asks them to leave the walking path clear. Her son carries away a basket of spent wicks and asks whether the two visitors tell the same story about the sleepers.

‘We remember it in our prayers,’ the neighbour says. The traveller adds, ‘I learned different names from my father.’ Mariam tells her son to listen to each guest before repeating either version. You ask whether the cave itself has always been called by that story. ‘The people who come here know it by that name,’ she says. ‘My family looks after the place they actually use.’ The child checks the shelves and makes room for one more light.

The visitors invite you to sit with them. Mariam can give you a sheltered place for the night if you want to stay and help tidy the cave in the morning.

## ev.cascar.entry.body

At a relay yard in Kashgar, a weaver’s son named Tariq is repairing a cord that records the handover of pack animals. His father has sent him a roll of cloth, but one knot has slipped beneath the seal. ‘If I count it as an animal, they will charge us for a horse we never had,’ Tariq says. The yard clerk makes him loosen the cord and show the older mark.

You ask where to find water before the next stage. Tariq points to a channel running by the market, then asks the clerk whether it is flowing today. ‘It is,’ she says, ‘but the far orchard gets its turn after noon.’ A fruit seller behind her has a sealed lot of jade for 135 silver, though he is more interested in whether his melons have reached the stall without bruising. Tariq asks him to move the crate out of the animal path.

The clerk invites you to stay two days and learn the relay’s records. Tariq can introduce you to his family’s garden tomorrow. The merchant pulls a sample from the jade pack while the repaired cord dries on the fence.

## ev.ev_cascar_consequence_b.title

If the turn changes, she must keep the wet bundles another night. Tariq arrives with his father’s note: he had mixed the day of the pale patch with the day the neighbour repaired the stone. Amina laughs at herself for nearly doing the same.

She sketches the water turns on a piece of cloth and asks whether you want to carry the corrected account. Her sister can still add the precise hour when she returns from the washing yard.

## ev.cascar.redthread.title

The Red Thread for Grandmother

## ev.cascar.redthread.body

Near the weaving quarter, a girl named Nura is trying to match a short red thread to a much older one sewn into her grandmother’s shawl. Her mother has asked her to repair the loose edge before Grandmother’s visit. The new thread looks right in the sun, but indoors it turns brighter than the old dye. ‘She will see it at once,’ Nura says. Her younger brother answers that Grandmother mostly notices whether they come to sit with her.

You ask where the old thread came from. Nura’s mother says her own mother dyed it many years ago and has mended the shawl several times since. She does not mind a visible seam; she would rather the cloth hold through the next cold season. Nura tests a stitch on the inner edge, then asks whether the thread should run all the way around or only across the worn part.

Their mother can teach you the repair as she works. If you stay for the day, Nura will also tell her grandmother who helped finish it. The younger brother has already pulled up an extra stool.
