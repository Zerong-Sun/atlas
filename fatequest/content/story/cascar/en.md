## city.cascar.name

Cascar

## ev.cascar.a.body

Cascar is the richest oasis on the Silk Road, where the melt-water from the Pamir and
the Karakorum pours down the mountain valleys to feed a green paradise in the heart of
the desert. Orchards of apricot, pomegranate, mulberry, walnut, and almond stretch for
miles along the irrigation canals; the vines grow so heavy with grapes that the
trellises must be replaced every year. The people are Muslims, but they keep many old
customs from before the time of the Prophet. The grapes of Cascar are dried into
raisins; the melons are the sweetest and largest in the world. A traveler who has
crossed the mountains and tasted the melons of Cascar for the first time will speak of
nothing else for a month.

## ev.cascar.b.body

The carpets of Cascar are woven so fine that a man can pass one through a finger-ring.
The women of the city work for months on a single carpet, drawing the pattern from
memory and knowing every dye by its smell: madder-root for red, indigo for blue,
pomegranate rind for yellow, walnut hull for brown, and a secret mineral from the
mountains for a green that fades only after a hundred years. The patterns are said to
contain prayers — a carpet woven with the right designs will protect its owner from the
evil eye, from snake-bite, and from the spirits of the desert. A merchant who buys a
Cascar carpet never travels without it, and the carpets that reach the courts of Europe
are treated as the treasure of kings.

## ev.cascar.c.body

Outside the city walls, in the face of a limestone cliff, there is a cave where seven
holy men are said to sleep. The Muslims say they have been sleeping since before the
time of the Prophet, waiting for the Day of Judgment. The Christians say they are their
own Seven Sleepers of Ephesus, miraculously transported here. A mosque and a chapel
stand side by side at the cave entrance, sharing the same cliff-face wall. Pilgrims of
both faiths come here, and neither side disturbs the other. The monks and the mullahs
take turns sweeping the cave; the offerings of candles, incense, and silver coins are
divided equally. It is said that anyone who stays in the cave overnight will hear the
breathing of the sleepers.

## ev.cascar.entry.body

Cascar is a region lying between north-east and east, and constituted a kingdom in
former days, but now it is subject to the Great Kaan. The people worship Mahommet. There
are a good number of towns and villages, but the greatest and finest is Cascar itself.
The inhabitants live by trade and handicrafts; they have beautiful gardens and
vineyards, and fine estates, and grow a great deal of cotton. From this country many
merchants go forth about the world on trading journeys. The natives are a wretched,
niggardly set of people; they eat and drink in miserable fashion. There are in the
country many Nestorian Christians, who have churches of their own. The people of the
country have a peculiar language, and the territory extends for five days’ journey.

## ev.ev_cascar_a_followup.title

Cascar: The Orchard Keeper's Seed

## ev.ev_cascar_a_followup.body

The keeper of the highest terrace meets you where the melt-water enters the garden, at the
head of the irrigation channel. He has tended these orchards for fifty years, and he knows
every tree by its fruit. He tells you how the water travels before it reaches Cascar: from
the snows of the Pamir, down the valley by the relay road, where horses are changed at each
station, and so into the canal that feeds the whole green belt of the city. Then he leads
you to a vine he keeps apart from the others, trained against the garden wall. Its seed, he
says, was carried over the mountains by his grandfather, who crossed by the same pass in a
year of snow. He will not sell you this vine. But he offers you the melon it bore this
season, and with it the story of the crossing that brought the seed here. The gardens of
Cascar keep their sweetness, he says, because the people remember where each seed came
from. What you take from his orchard, and what you carry of the road that waters it, is
yours to choose.

Take the keeper's melon and hear the story of its seed

## ev.ev_cascar_a_followup.choice_1_result

The keeper cuts the melon at the water's edge and gives you the largest piece. Its sweetness
stays with you for the rest of the day, and the story of the crossing goes into your travel

## ev.ev_cascar_b_followup.title

Cascar: The Dyer's Border

## ev.ev_cascar_b_followup.body

The dyer who matched every colour for the carpet bazaar calls you into her yard behind the
market, where the dye-pots stand in a row. She asks what you saw in the carpets, and when
you answer she nods, as though you had passed some test. She takes up the border of an
unfinished carpet and reads it aloud: the pattern is a prayer, she says, folded into the
wool so that a house might live inside it. She knows every colour by smell — madder-root,
indigo, pomegranate rind, walnut hull — and for the green she keeps a secret. The mineral
that makes it is dug two days' road to the south, in the hills above the plain, and only two
families in Cascar know the pit. She offers you the dye-stuffs she has ready in the yard,
and the reading of the border, and the road to the green pit. A carpet, she says, keeps its
owner safe only as long as the weaver's intention is carried with it; a prayer folded into
wool is still a prayer. Choose what you will carry.

## ev.ev_cascar_c_followup.title

Cascar: The Names of the Sleepers

## ev.ev_cascar_c_followup.body

At the cave mouth, the monk of the chapel and the mullah of the mosque meet you together,
and the two faiths stand side by side as they always do in the stone. They have heard that
you listened through the night. Each offers you the names of the seven sleepers — and the
two lists are not the same. The mullah gives you the names as they are written in his book;
the monk gives you the names of the sleepers of Ephesus. Both men insist that the cave holds
the same seven men. They ask which road brought you to Cascar, and when you answer, the monk
tells you of the pilgrim road from the east, by which the story of the sleepers was carried
from one faith to the other, and of the oasis of Cotan, where both tales are told in the
same market. The offerings at the cave are divided equally, and so, they say, is the truth.
They leave the choice to you: take both names, take the road, or keep the night's vigil.

## ev.ev_cascar_c_followup.choice_1_result

The monk writes one list and the mullah writes another, and both press their papers into
your hand. You carry the seven sleepers under two names now, and the cave keeps both.

## ev.ev_cascar_c_followup.choice_2

## ev.ev_cascar_c_followup.choice_2_result

## ev.ev_cascar_mentor_followup.title

Cascar: The Relay Keeper's Cord

## ev.ev_cascar_mentor_followup.body

The guide of the mountain relay does not let you leave Cascar with only his account. He
unwinds from his wrist a length of cord, knotted at intervals, and lays it on the stone
between you. Each knot, he says, is a relay station between Cascar and the pass; a man who
cannot read can still count the road by it. In his youth he carried the government post
across the mountains, sleeping at each relay and changing his horse in the dark, and the
cord is the only map he ever needed. He speaks of the roads that leave the city as though
they were his children: the way the post climbs to the pass, the way the traders take west
to Samarcanda, and the way the caravan comes up from the south. He offers you the cord, and
the naming of the roads, and a day of his patience. Choose what will serve you best, he
says, because a road is only as good as what you carry into it.

## ev.ev_cascar_mentor_followup.choice_1_result

The guide slips the knotted cord over your wrist. You carry the relays of Cascar with you
now, and the road they count will not run out from under you.
