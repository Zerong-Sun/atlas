## city.baldacum.name

Baudas

## ev.baldacum.a.body

At an old palace wing by the river, a keeper named Yusuf is clearing silt from a narrow water channel. The courtyard has been repaired more than once, and he knows which stones belong to which stretch of work. His granddaughter has brought a basket for the loose tiles. ‘Only the broken ones,’ he tells her. She holds up a whole blue tile and asks why it lies beside the ditch.

‘Because someone set it aside when the water ran too high,’ Yusuf says. You ask where the channel reaches the Tigris. He points through two courtyards and says the same river carries cargo south toward Basra. A metal merchant is waiting at the gate with another full bundle of Damascus steel, priced at 146 silver; he has come to use the dry path through the yard.

The girl puts the whole tile back where her grandfather can find it. Yusuf asks whether you will walk the channels with him, look at the merchant’s steel, or sit while he finishes the work and talks about the old rooms. He lifts another stone and finds water still moving beneath it.

## ev.baldacum.b.body

In the Baghdad market, a weaver named Mariam is checking a brocade length for a customer. Her apprentice has left one gold thread loose near the edge. ‘I can mend it here,’ the boy says. She asks him to show her where he would begin. He touches the wrong row, and she puts a needle in his hand before pointing to the back of the cloth.

A money changer next door offers fruit syrup while they work. He tells you a pepper seller is waiting for word from Hormuz, while dates arrive from Basra by river. You ask whether his coins from those places look alike. He lays two on the table and says he weighs each before entering a value in his book.

Mariam’s full merchant length of brocade costs 135 silver. The apprentice finally finds the loose end and asks her to hold the cloth still. ‘Now you can mend it,’ she says. The money changer pushes a cup toward you and tells him to make the stitch before the drink warms.

## ev.baldacum.c.body

In a tea house, an older storyteller has set out three scraps of paper. Each records what a family told him about the siege years ago. One speaks of a broken riverbank, another of a gate crowded with people, and a third of someone carrying a small chest out of a burning house. He asks his daughter to read the names aloud so he does not mix the families up.

‘That one was my mother’s neighbour,’ she says. You ask whether the storyteller has checked the dates. ‘As far as the records let me,’ he answers, ‘but one man’s grandfather changed the story each time he told it.’ A listener at the next table points out that his family remembers a different gate. The daughter offers him a blank page rather than arguing.

A dealer beside the storyteller turns an old coin over and says he will sell it for 2 silver. The daughter has two days of visits planned with relatives whose accounts are still missing. The storyteller holds the blank page toward the listener and asks for the first name.

## ev.baldacum.c.title

Three Accounts of the Siege

## ev.baldacum.entry.body

At the Tigris landing, a boatman and his sister are arguing over a wet cargo list. She handles their family’s accounts; he left the paper under a water jar while unloading. Now one line of numbers has blurred. ‘Tell me which bundle it was before I write anything,’ she says. He points to a stack of cloth and swears it was the smallest one.

A clerk from the customs desk comes over to check the count. The boatman asks him to wait while his sister compares the list with the marks on the bundles. You hold the top sheet out of the spray. A merchant beside them is selling a full bundle of Damascus steel for 146 silver; he wants the clerk to finish so he can load it before dark.

You ask who knows the city’s streets and older stories. The sister says the river workers know the landings, the merchants know the markets, and an old keeper near a former palace wing will tell you how the water channels were laid out. She finds the blurred number on the third bundle and taps it with her finger. ‘There. Write that one down.’

## ev.baldacum.boatplug.title

The Boatman’s Wooden Plug

## ev.baldacum.boatplug.body

At a small landing, a boatman has pulled his boat onto the bank to mend a leak. His daughter brings a wooden plug her grandfather shaped, but it is too wide for the hole. The boatman asks her to fetch his knife. ‘Grandfather said to cut the edge, not the middle,’ she replies, guarding the plug in both hands.

You help hold the hull while he works. A neighbour waiting to cross asks when the boat will float again. ‘When my daughter says the plug fits,’ the boatman says. She rolls her eyes, then presses the piece into place and checks whether it sits level with the planks. You ask where the neighbour is going. She has a bundle of letters for family on the far bank and can wait until the repair is sound.

The boatman offers to pay you 1 silver if you stay the day and help test the hull. If you are leaving soon, he can instead show you how the plug is cut and sealed; his daughter has already laid the knife and cloth beside the boat.
