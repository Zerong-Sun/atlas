## city.balc.name

Balc

## ev.balc.a.body

By an older wall in Balkh, mason Farid is sorting loose stones for a neighbour’s doorway. His niece Mina holds two pieces with the same flower carved on them. ‘They belong together,’ she says. Farid sets them side by side and finds that one is a different thickness. ‘They may have stood in different places. Bring the level before we build either into the door.’

You ask who made the carving. Farid does not know; his father told stories of earlier houses here, and he has repaired this lane all his life. Mina asks whether people will still see the flower when the stones are set. ‘If we turn one toward the street,’ he says. A caravan woman passing with a basket mentions that her family has sent workmen from Samarkand before. She can speak about that road when her delivery is done.

Farid asks you to help measure the doorway. Mina finds a small carved fragment that cannot bear weight; her uncle says you may keep it if you want to remember the work. The woman returns from her delivery and waits while Mina holds up the level.

## ev.balc.a.title

Stones Beside the Old Wall

## ev.balc.b.body

In a caravan inn, the keeper’s son Yunus is checking two ledgers that got wet at the trough. One belongs to a lapis trader from Badakhshan, the other to a woman taking cloth toward Samarkand. ‘You put my pack number under his stones,’ she tells him. Yunus finds the mark and apologises; he had copied it while a camel pushed against the table. The lapis trader laughs and moves his chest away from the water.

You ask why the traders do not simply mark the chests again. The woman says a seal shows who paid for carriage, and a new mark would require another witness. Yunus calls his mother from the kitchen to compare the old ink. The trader opens his chest: one full lot of lapis costs 135 silver. He also has a single uncut balas ruby for the same price. ‘Buy if you like,’ he says, ‘but let her sign first.’

His mother confirms the pack number. The woman with cloth is ready to show you the two roads when Yunus returns the ledgers, and he brings a clean sheet for your notes.

## ev.balc.c.body

On a low hill outside Balkh, an old keeper is sheltering a small shrine lamp from the wind. His grandson has brought fresh oil but left the wick at home. ‘I thought there would still be one here,’ the boy says. The keeper asks him to cut a strip from the spare cloth. When you offer your knife, the boy takes it carefully and gives it back with the blade clean.

You ask whose shrine it is. The keeper says families from different roads have named it differently for many years; his own family tends the lamp because his mother asked him to. ‘Did she tell you the old stories too?’ the boy asks. ‘More than I can remember,’ he replies. A woman visiting the hill sets down a small dish of incense, pays for oil, and asks whether she may rest until morning. The keeper shows her a sheltered place beside the wall.

He offers you the same shelter if you want to stay a night. The boy lights the new wick and waits to hear whether you would rather sit with them at dusk or come back at dawn.

## ev.balc.c.title

The Keeper of the Hill Shrine

## ev.balc.entry.body

At the toll station outside Balkh, a clerk is trying to dry a page on which three caravan names have run together. His daughter Laila has set a flat stone on one corner, but the wind keeps lifting the other. ‘If I write over it now, Father, the next man will swear he paid twice.’ The clerk asks her to fetch an older list. You hold the page while she looks.

You ask which road is easiest to provision. The clerk points to his store of empty water jars. ‘That depends on the wells and the season. Ask the drivers before you leave.’ Laila returns with the list and notices that one caravan carried a sealed lot of jade. A merchant behind you says he still has one to sell for 135 silver. She asks whether the mark beside its name means it came through Samarkand. Her father says the driver can answer that when he comes back.

As the ink dries, the clerk invites you to stay and learn how the toll records work. Laila wants to show you the family’s small collection of old stones after her work is done. The jade merchant lays a sample beside the register. Laila turns the page away from it before the ink can stain the stone.

Umar writes the correction, then asks whether you would carry a note to the next record keeper if your roads meet. Laila reminds him to put the family name on the outside so nobody opens the note merely to learn who it is for.

Karim wants to make a copy of the letters first. He has brought paper and charcoal, though the bowl is slippery in his hands. Laila steadies it and asks whether you want to see the marks with them.

## ev.balc.threadspool.title

The Rope Maker’s Spool

## ev.balc.threadspool.body

In a yard behind the market, rope maker Hana is teaching her son Idris to wind a long line around a wooden spool. A neighbour needs it to draw water from a deep well; Idris has tied one end with a knot too large for the iron ring. ‘Try it against the ring before you finish the whole length,’ Hana says. He pulls the knot through, and it catches exactly where she expected.

You ask whether the line is too thick. Hana says the fibres are sound; the knot needs to sit closer to the end. Idris asks why she had him make it before showing him. ‘You remembered the one for the cart,’ she says. ‘This well uses a narrower ring.’ He laughs, unties it, and lets you feel where the twist is tight. Their neighbour arrives with the ring and a bucket, eager to try the rope before evening.

Hana offers to pay you 0.8 silver if you stay and help finish several spools for her orders. Idris can also show you the well test now; he holds the iron ring steady and waits for the new knot.
