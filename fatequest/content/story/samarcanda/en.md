## city.samarcanda.name

Samarcanda

## ev.samarcanda.a.body

Beside a canal in Samarcand, teacher Hamid is waiting for his niece to return with a book. She arrives wet to the knees and says she stopped to help a gardener lift a fallen branch from the water. Hamid asks whether the book stayed dry. She opens the cloth around it and shows him. A student under the tiled porch offers to read the next passage aloud while she dries her shoes.

You ask what they are studying. Hamid says they compare the hour of a journey by the sun and by reports from travellers. His niece says the gardener knows the canal’s turns better than any book, and Hamid invites him to join the discussion. The gardener asks whether the students can first help him clear the branch fully from the bank. The girl is already rolling up her sleeves.

The porch has a blue-tiled dome above one room; farther along, the canal reaches a small garden. Hamid keeps the lesson open for two days, and the gardener promises to show visitors where the water goes once the branch is moved.

## ev.samarcanda.b.body

In a paper yard, maker Yusuf is pressing a sheet while his daughter Mariam counts the dry ones. A calligrapher arrives with a page from an illuminated manuscript and says his sister’s letter must be copied before he binds the book. Mariam says the sister will want a reply sooner than the book will be ready. Yusuf agrees and gives her a clean sheet.

You ask why the paper feels so smooth. Yusuf shows how he polishes a dry sheet with a stone. Mariam stops him before he touches one still damp at the edge. ‘That one is for the letter,’ she says. The calligrapher asks if she has already decided what to write. She says she will tell her sister about the new apprentice who keeps leaving his sandals in the drying yard; the boy hears her and moves them at once.

Yusuf sells a full paper trading bale for 100 silver. The calligrapher has a bound decorated manuscript for 10 silver. Mariam asks whether you would like to watch the paper work before they set the letter down.

## ev.samarcanda.c.body

In a mosque courtyard, caretaker Rashid is wiping rain from a dark stone beside the wall. His daughter wants to leave one drop so her grandmother can see it. ‘She has seen plenty of rain,’ he says. The girl replies that grandmother will still ask whether this stone caught any. Rashid lets her keep the small wet patch until the older woman arrives.

You ask why people watch the stone. Rashid says neighbours have told stories about it for years; some say the dampness came before troubled seasons, others bring family news when they visit. His mother arrives with a folded cloth and asks the girl to show her the drop. She touches the stone, then asks Rashid whether he has mended the leak in the roof above. ‘That is the part I can answer,’ he says, pointing to new mortar.

The family is staying for the evening prayer. Rashid has an old notebook of dated weather and repairs; his mother says she remembers one of those wet seasons differently. They make space for you at the bench.

## ev.samarcanda.entry.body

At the gate of Samarcand, a clerk named Shirin is sorting travel permits while her younger brother carries a roll of paper to their father’s shop. He asks whether he may keep one clean sheet for a letter. Shirin says their father has already set one aside, if the boy remembers to take the parcel to the right house. He points to the blue-tiled roof beyond the lane, sure he knows the way.

You ask Shirin where to declare your goods. She shows you the desk and says the count will take two days if you join the line. Her brother says he knows a family in the market who can explain the city’s different communities and the stories their elders tell. ‘Ask them yourself,’ Shirin says. ‘He leaves out the parts where he got lost.’ The boy protests that he only took the wrong bridge once.

A merchant opens a full jade trading parcel nearby, asking 135 silver. Shirin notes the seller’s name before turning back to you; her brother has finally picked up the paper roll and is waiting for someone to show him the bridge on the map.

## ev.ev_samarcanda_consequence_a.title

The Address on Shirin’s List

## ev.ev_samarcanda_consequence_a.body

The student who waited near Shirin’s desk catches up with you in the lane. His name is Nadir, and he has been asked to carry a note to an older teacher who remembers the street names used before the last flood shifted a canal. The writing on the note is faint. ‘Shirin says you copied the market lanes,’ he tells you. ‘Can you help me check which bridge this means?’

You ask why he does not take it straight to the teacher. Nadir says he already tried one house; its owner invited him in for tea but had never heard of the teacher. His little sister is waiting at home for him to return with a book the teacher promised her. He shows you the name beside the address, then admits he copied one letter badly.

A neighbour steps out to shake a mat. Nadir asks her whether she knows the old bridge name. She does, but wants to see the note before sending you across town. Nadir holds it up in the light and waits.

The family invites you to take the corrected sketch now. If you stay another day, you can copy the old and new street names into your book with the teacher’s help.

## ev.ev_samarcanda_consequence_b.title

The Paper Maker’s Two Lists

## ev.ev_samarcanda_consequence_b.body

After you have heard the neighbours’ stories, paper maker Yusuf finds you in the market with two lists in his hand. One names the caravan that carried his sister’s letter west; the other names a party who brought linen rags for his workshop. The same driver appears on both lists, but the departure dates differ. ‘Mariam says I copied one of them wrong,’ he tells you. ‘She is probably right.’

You ask why the dates matter. Yusuf says his sister promised to send news of their mother, and Mariam keeps asking when a reply might come. He does not want to tell her to wait for the wrong caravan. An old porter at the next stall remembers the driver but not the date. Yusuf asks him whether the man carried paper or cloth on his first trip. The porter can answer that much.

Yusuf sits down to compare the lists. He offers you a clean copy once they agree on the order, or you can leave the family to settle it together.

## ev.ev_samarcanda_consequence_b_resolution.title

A Reply Worth Waiting For

## ev.ev_samarcanda_consequence_b_resolution.body

Mariam has found her father’s mistake: he put the cloth caravan’s return date beside the letter caravan’s departure. Yusuf reads the corrected line aloud and asks her whether she will now stop asking about the reply every morning. ‘I will ask on the right mornings,’ she says. The old porter, back for paper, laughs so hard he almost drops his parcel.

You ask whether the letter caravan used the west gate. The porter tells you which yard it left from, and Yusuf adds that name to the paper. Mariam asks him to include the driver’s name too; he did promise to look after her aunt’s reply. She copies the whole entry with a steadier hand than her father’s.

They offer to share the corrected dates with the next student going west. You can tell that traveller now, or spend a day making a careful copy for your own route notes.

## ev.samarcanda.mentor_closure.title

The Older Student’s Map

## ev.samarcanda.mentor_closure.body

Nadir’s older fellow student, Bashir, is drawing a street map for a young mason whose family has moved across the canal. He marks the bridges, then asks the mason to trace the route back with his finger. The young man misses a turn. Bashir erases that line and redraws it wider. ‘A map is for the person who has to walk it,’ he says.

You ask how he learned to draw so clearly. Bashir says he spent years carrying books between teachers and asking the people on each street what they called it. His sister, a paper seller, comes by and points out that one bridge has been repaired since his last drawing. He asks her for the date and adds it beside the line. The mason now finds his new home on the page.

Bashir offers to show you his method in a short conversation, spend a full day walking the route, or let you carry on. His sister leaves a clean scrap of paper for the next map.

## ev.samarcanda.bluetile.title

The Tile That Went Missing

## ev.samarcanda.bluetile.body

At a tile yard, Safiya is fitting a few blue pieces onto a bench her father built for the family courtyard. One piece is missing from the stack. Her cousin thinks a buyer took it by mistake. Safiya asks him which buyer came today; he names only the paper seller, who never entered the yard. Her father suggests checking the shaded drying shelf before accusing anyone.

You ask why this bench needs a particular pattern. Safiya says her grandmother chose it: a little blue line to show where each child once sat while waiting for bread. The grandmother comes out carrying lunch and laughs when she sees them searching. She had moved the tile under the shelf to keep rain from staining the unfired back. Her grandson fetches it and admits he should have checked there first.

Safiya has the piece again, but the bench still needs a day’s careful setting. The grandmother asks you to sit and hear why she chose the blue line before anyone starts work with wet mortar.
