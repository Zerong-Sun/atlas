## ev.camadi.entry.title

Camadi at the Edge of the Plain

## ev.camadi.entry.body

Camadi stands on a hot plain at the foot of the hills. Old walls stand beside lived-in houses, and date palms shade a row of stalls. A fruit seller named Banu sorts dates while her grandson searches the basket for a pistachio he dropped. Nearby, a merchant asks whether she has any of the fruit travellers call ‘apples of paradise.’

‘Leave that nut,’ Banu tells her grandson. ‘The birds will find it before you do.’ She gives him another, then shows the merchant what remains of the fruit. She tells you where to refill water before crossing the plain. She remembers when more families lived beyond the old walls, and she still knows the names of the lanes there.

A mule driver asks Banu whether her old lane still has a roofed place to rest. ‘The roof is gone,’ she says, ‘but my cousin’s house is beside it. Tell him I sent you.’ Her grandson asks why everyone comes to her for directions. Banu hands him a date and says, ‘Because I have sent them all to your cousin at least once.’ You ask how far the next dependable water is. She sets down the basket to point it out rather than guessing while she works.

## ev.camadi.a.title

Banu’s Old Street

## ev.camadi.a.body

Banu leads her grandson through a broken arch. ‘Your great-grandfather kept his dates over there,’ she says, pointing to a patch of wall. The boy asks why they moved. She tells him that the old quarter was damaged in fighting, and the family built again closer to the water.

There is still a path through the ruins toward the Kerman road. Banu will walk it with you, sell you a small bag of dates to eat on the journey, or introduce you to caravan people who know the way toward Cobinan.

The boy asks whether the old house had the same door as theirs now. Banu shows him two stone sockets where a wooden door once turned. ‘Your great-grandfather could hear a camel coming before he saw it,’ she says. You ask what he sold. ‘Mostly dates. If a neighbour had no fruit that week, he let her use the cool room to store hers.’ A caravan driver passing the arch calls Banu by name and asks whether the Kerman turn is still open. She offers to walk there with you and show you.

## ev.camadi.b.title

A Hot Afternoon by the Canal

## ev.camadi.b.body

A narrow channel feeds Banu’s date palms. Her neighbour covers a basket of fruit against the sun, while two travellers share water under the trees. Beside the fruit stall is a bale of cotton cloth packed for a merchant caravan. One traveller asks where the inland road reaches Hormuz, the port for ships toward Calatu.

Banu says she can name the road to Hormuz and its onward sea passage toward Calatu, as well as the road back to Kerman. You may rest with the travellers for the day or buy the full bale of cloth from the neighbour.

‘Do the boats leave every day?’ the traveller asks. Banu shakes her head. ‘Ask the sailors when you reach the port. I can tell you the road, not their departure.’ Her neighbour lifts the corner of the cotton bale and asks whether you want to see the weave before buying the whole lot. You ask if the cloth has travelled far already. ‘Only from the fields to my loom,’ she says. ‘Your caravan would take it farther than I have.’

## ev.ev_camadi_a_followup.title

A Map under Banu’s Palm

## ev.ev_camadi_a_followup.body

Banu spreads a worn cloth under her palm and draws the old quarter as she remembers it. Her grandson adds the well and the house they live in now. ‘That part is your map,’ she tells him.

She has a caravan lot of dates ready to sell to traders at a fixed price. You can buy the lot, ask her how travellers reach Hormuz and take ship toward Calatu, or stay a day and help the boy put the family’s names in the right places on the cloth.

The boy puts his finger on a blank space in the cloth. ‘Who lived here?’ Banu names a baker and then remembers his daughter has a stall by the present market. You ask whether she will show the daughter this drawing. ‘When it is finished,’ she says. ‘She may remember where her father kept the oven.’ Another trader arrives to ask about the date lot but has not agreed to buy it. Banu looks up at you and asks whether you want to discuss the price first.

## ev.camadi.oldwell.title

The Well behind the Date Trees

## ev.camadi.oldwell.body

Behind a row of date palms, two siblings are clearing stones from a well mouth. Their mother remembers drawing water there as a child. The younger one has already hauled out three baskets of gravel and wants to stop; the elder thinks they can finish before the next caravan arrives.

Their mother asks what help you can give. You can spend the day clearing the last stones with them, or walk to the caravan yard and bring the water carriers back to help finish the work.

The younger sibling asks how much more stone they must move. His sister lowers a rope and lets him hear it touch water. ‘Only the top is blocked,’ she says. Their mother laughs at how seriously they have taken a well she once thought abandoned. You ask whether other people may use it when they finish. ‘Of course,’ she answers. ‘That is why the water carriers should know about it.’ The brother looks toward the caravan yard and wonders whether anyone there will lend them a stronger basket.

## ev.camadi.oldwell.result.clear

You work with the family until the well can be used comfortably. Their mother draws the first bucket and shares the cool water with everyone. After a day’s labour and rest, you feel refreshed.

## ev.camadi.oldwell.result.tell

At the caravan yard, the water carriers listen and follow you back to the palms. They help move the last heavy stones, and the family thanks you for bringing them. Word of your help spreads through Camadi.
