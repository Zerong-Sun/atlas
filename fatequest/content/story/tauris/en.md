## city.tauris.name

## ev.tauris.a.body

Pearl trader Narin has laid a dark cloth over his table so his daughter can see which stones differ in colour. She picks one from a bowl and asks why it is kept apart. ‘Because a customer left it while deciding,’ he says. The daughter puts it back and asks when that customer is coming. Narin admits he does not know, so he writes her name beside the bowl.

You ask about a group of merchants arguing over two roads. One has come from Baldacum with a damaged pack frame; another is waiting for news from Trapezus before sending cloth west. Narin calls them over and asks each to tell you the first reliable stopping place. They disagree about the weather, then discover they travelled in different seasons. His daughter adds the season to both notes.

A full pearl trade parcel costs 135 silver. The hill above the market is open to walk, and the merchants can point out the roads from there. Narin folds the dark cloth over the waiting customer’s pearl before the dust reaches it.

## ev.tauris.b.body

On a bench near the covered stalls, interpreter Mariam is helping a cloth seller understand a visitor’s order. The visitor speaks Italian, the seller answers in Persian, and Mariam asks them both to repeat the number of bolts. The seller’s apprentice tries to help by holding up his fingers, but he counts the sample cloth as a full bolt. Mariam takes the sample from him and lets him count again.

You ask how she learned to work between traders. She says her mother kept rooms for travellers and made every child listen at supper. Her own daughter now copies new words into a notebook. A western merchant says he knows the road back toward Constantinopolis; another traveller has just returned from the north and mentions Caffa. Mariam invites the first man to sit, so nobody has to shout across the stall.

She can teach you Persian over twenty days for 20 silver. For now she is making the apprentice read the order back to both customers, one language at a time.

## ev.tauris.entry.body

At a covered market in Tauris, steel seller Farah is arguing gently with her brother over a ledger. He has written the weight of a full trade bale of Damascus steel on the line for a much smaller order. ‘If I send that page to the tax desk, they’ll wonder why the mule is carrying so little,’ she says. Her brother lifts the heavier bale and admits he copied from the wrong tag. Farah puts both tags side by side and asks him to read them aloud.

You ask where new arrivals settle their goods. Farah says the office takes two days to check a large load, while her brother can show you the public well and the road into the market. A porter tells you that the old houses beyond the well have stories from many rulers and many rebuildings; his grandmother remembers where one lane used to bend before a workshop moved there.

Farah’s complete bale of steel is for sale at 145.8 silver. Her brother rewrites the ledger, and she asks you to check the weight once more before she seals it.

Near the caravan lodgings, a widow named Lucia is sorting a set of painted cards while her nephew repairs the box. He asks why two cards show travellers leaving by different roads. ‘Because the painter could not fit both roads on one card,’ she says. He calls that a poor excuse, and she asks him to draw a better one.

You ask what she does with the cards. Lucia says she uses them to teach visitors to tell a choice clearly: what they hope for, what they can afford, and whom the choice will affect. Her nephew says some travellers come back only to tell her what happened next. ‘And you always listen?’ you ask. ‘When they bring supper,’ she says, and the nephew groans because he has been carrying the bowls himself. She puts the repaired box beside her and asks what you want to know.

At the next table a teacher sets marked star dice beside a small diagram. Lucia’s nephew asks him to roll quietly while they finish talking; the teacher grins and catches the dice in his palm.
